Physically based light, in your browser

A WebGPU path tracer that renders in real units, candela per square metre and lux, with a scene builder, a measuring tool, and an optics bench for LED and luminaire design that traces the same scene the picture shows. Nothing to install and no server: it runs on your own graphics card.

Who makes it, and why

I'm Matthijs Keuper: an applied physicist from the University of Twente, an engineer and an artist. For three decades I have turned hard technical problems into products. I founded Invisua Lighting and ran it for ten years: a tunable LED lighting system, its optics, colour algorithms and firmware included, that ended up in flagship stores for Apple, BMW and Selfridges. Today I work on LED systems and nonimaging optics at Keuper Innovations, and as an artist at code+wood: wood reliefs, engravings and generative work where software, lasers and robots meet traditional techniques.

ShadyTracer is where the two meet. I always wanted a ray tracer that did what I wanted from one: light in the units an optical engineer measures it in, and pictures worth looking at. This is it.

How it is made

I designed ShadyTracer and I decide what it does and how it should work. Most of its code is written by Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, under my direction: I set the goals, review what comes back and measure it on real hardware. I say so openly, because it is a fair question. What should decide whether you trust a renderer is not who typed it but what checks it: every ✓ on this page is a measurement against a closed-form answer on a real graphics card, more than a hundred test files run on every change, and FEATURES.md gives the evidence for every feature, including what has not been confirmed yet.

Why sponsor

ShadyTracer is free and stays free. Sponsoring pays for hardware: everything measured so far ran on two NVIDIA cards, and the renderer should be checked on others. And it tells me the work is useful to someone, which is worth as much.

What it does

A renderer that treats light as something to measure, and a workshop to build what it lights. ✓ checked against a closed-form answer on a real graphics card · preview built and tested without one, not yet confirmed on one · unmarked: in use.

Light found the way light goes

  • Wavefront path tracing on the GPU, with next-event estimation and multiple importance sampling toward the sun and sky: 3.8× less noise, unbiased ✓
  • Caustics through glass, by tracing photons from the sun and learning where they are worth sending ✓; and what is seen through glass or in a mirror, lit right, by a photon map.
  • Any glowing surface is a light, with nothing declared ✓
  • Dust in the air: a haze box whose air scatters light in real units, so a beam shows in it; it loses no light, to 0.02 % ✓
  • Glass the way glass behaves: dispersion, dichroic and anti-reflection coatings, and a camera lens with depth of field preview

Light as numbers

Luminance in cd/m² and illuminance in lux, everywhere. A white floor under sun and sky reads its textbook value to 0.1 % ✓. The sun is its illuminance at its real size, the sky the light it puts on the ground.

  • Lux and luminance as false colour, with an error bar on every pixel preview
  • A measure tool: pins and areas read lux, uniformity U0 and colour temperature off any surface, a ruler measures distances, all of it as CSV preview

Every shape is code

The scene builder makes shapes from signed distance fields, each one code you can edit, with compile errors shown at your own line and column. Move handles in the picture, CSG, modifiers, undo; projects of several scenes, kept in your browser as they change.

  • STEP and STL parts imported as shapes, exact, with analytic normals preview
  • 3MF with colour for multi-colour 3D printing, straight from the scene preview

Surfaces with relief

Plaster, knockdown stucco, sand-float render, rough stone, hammered metal, leather, orange-peel paint: a noise library for surfaces (value, gradient, fBm and Worley noise) displaces them, each noise with a proved or measured bound on its slope, so a displaced surface never lets the march step through it preview.

Any material field can be code; brushed, turned and radial grain along the surface ✓.

A library to start from

43 built-in items (shapes, modifiers, lights and material presets) and 42 named materials, from plastics, paints and plaster to brushed, anodised and hammered metal, clear, tinted, frosted and flint glass, stone, wood and leather. Five optics parts too: a PMMA lens, an LED die, a detector screen, a prism and a whole LED–lens–screen bench preview. Keep your own items beside them, and drag any of them onto the tree or into the picture preview.

Animation and video

Keyframe any live setting (the camera, the sun, a light's flux, an object's position) and render it frame by frame to MP4 or GIF, each frame offscreen at the export size with its own samples preview.

For lighting and optics design

An optics bench over the same scene: a non-sequential Monte Carlo trace through the geometry and materials the picture uses, so the numbers and the picture cannot disagree. Rays are weighted, never discarded; every lumen is booked, emitted = escaped + lost + absorbed, to zero; and every number carries its standard error.

  • Refraction, Fresnel and total internal reflection match the textbook within two standard errors ✓
  • Sources: rectangle and disc lights in lumens or cd/m², Lambertian, cosⁿ, cones and collimated beams; colour as RGB, CCT + Duv or CIE xy; Zemax ray files preview
  • The optics sources light the picture at their real brightness: a lens's spot on a screen shows in lux ✓
  • See the rays, retraced live while you move things; Live analysis that follows every edit preview
  • Far field: intensity in cd or cd/klm on a type C grid, beam and field angles, I max, efficiency, zonal flux, a polar diagram preview
  • Illuminance maps in lux on a detector: isolux lines, profiles, U0 and Ud, encircled flux, a virtual plane at any distance preview
  • Colour: CIE xy, u′v′, CCT and Duv, Δu′v′ uniformity, single lights exact to 10⁻⁸ ✓; colour mixing to a target
  • Export: the intensity table, the illuminance map and the hits as CSV, any chart as PNG

Questions

What do I need to run it?

A browser with WebGPU: Chrome or Edge 113 or later, Firefox 141 or later on Windows, or Safari 26 or later. And a real graphics card: the results here were measured on NVIDIA cards. The line under the button at the top of this page says whether your browser can run it.

Do I need to install anything?

No. Open the app and it runs in the browser, on your own graphics card. There is no account, no download and no server doing the work.

Where is my work kept?

In your browser, on this computer, saved as it changes: a reload comes back to the same scene, camera and settings. The Project panel lists your projects and downloads any of them, and File › Export saves a scene or a whole project as a file. Clearing the browser's data for this site deletes them, so keep a file of anything you care about.

Is anything uploaded?

No. Your scenes stay in your browser. The page loads its own files from this site and sends nothing anywhere: no account, no analytics, no tracking.

Does it work on a phone or tablet?

It is made for a desktop or laptop with a graphics card: the interface is a workbench of docked panels, and path tracing wants a graphics card's power. A phone browser with WebGPU may start it, but that is not what it is built or tested for.

Is it free?

Yes. ShadyTracer is open source under the MIT licence, and the code is on GitHub. If it is useful to you, a tip through GitHub Sponsors keeps it going.

What do ✓ and preview mean on this page?

✓ means checked against a closed-form answer on a real graphics card, with the test that did it. Preview means built and tested without a graphics card, and not yet confirmed on one. Anything unmarked is in use.
