Raytracer UI: four approaches, and the one chosen

Rough mockups of the whole application, laid out four ways. Each one holds everything the program has today (as of 2026-09-27; FEATURES.md is the inventory now), plus what the roadmap will need: a pinned reference, a study of runs, export. The reasoning is in UI_PLAN.md.

The charts are the program's own code (polar.js, cartesian.js, heatmap.js, xychart.js) drawing synthetic data. The picture is a sketch, so no GPU is needed. Buttons mostly do nothing; tabs and menus open.

Chosen: E · Shell

A's docking, with presets that give B's simplicity, a real menu bar, pop-out windows for a second monitor, a library of objects and materials, charts that switch between flux and colour, help from every control, and every value addressable by a path for the timeline and a later optimiser. It works: drag, float, pop out, switch layouts, save your own, Ctrl K.

The reasoning and the architecture behind it are sections 6 and 7 of UI_PLAN.md.

Open E

Built: F · Analyse

The charts rearranged, built on the application's own shell, tables and charts: every chart a panel of its own, drawn edge to edge, sized by the dock. One Analysis pane holds the data every chart is made from, the selected chart's own settings (click a chart, as a row in the tree selects an object), and the results as cards. Point at a chart to read it; its siblings follow. Drag on the map to zoom.

Open F

Decided: G · Sections -- B, boxes, built

The Analysis pane's sections (Intensity, Map, Chart, Results) all had the same small muted heading and ran together. The real pane, five times on made-up data: as it is, and four ways to tell the parts apart -- A bands across the pane, B a box each, C a coloured rule by role (what goes in, how it is shown, what comes out), D numbered steps in the order of the work.

Open G

Proposed: H · Scenes, projects and the menus

The File menu had become the only place a project's scenes could be seen -- as names, in a submenu -- among 19 rows of projects, scenes, files and a picture; and the Render settings menu said again what the view's buttons and the Render settings pane already do. On one made-up project: today, and four ways to see the scenes over one floor they share -- File in 6 rows, no Render settings or Window menu, and a Project view (the projects, the scenes with their pictures, the examples) in place of the Projects dialog and File › Examples. A the view alone, B a quick list off the scene's row, C a strip of scene cards over the tree, D the whole project in the tree, the other scenes one dimmed line each. The menus, row by row, are its last tab. Menus open, scenes switch with a simulated compile, objects drag between scenes; the pictures are sketches.

The analysis, the options and the questions for you: section 11 of UI_PLAN.md.

Open H

The loop the UI has to serve

Modelgeometry, materials, lights
Lookthe picture, rays over it, traced live
Measuredetectors, the far field, a plane h away
AnalyseE, U0, beam, efficiency, each ± its error
Decidechange a parameter; compare with the last runs

…and round again, many times, then Report. The hard case: tune a lens to make a detector's illuminance uniform. That needs the parameter, the detector's map, U0 and what it trades against, the previous runs and a small 3D view all on screen together. Today the parameter is in the Builder page and the map covers the 3D view, and there is no record of previous runs at all.

The four

A Workbench

like VS Code, an IDE, KiCad

A menu bar, an activity bar of modules, and a docking area: every view is a tab you can drag, split and stack. Each tab is labelled with the module it belongs to.

  • Any arrangement: parameter, map, runs and view side by side
  • The module split is visible in every tab
  • Grows without redesign: a new chart is a new tab
  • A person has to arrange it; a new user sees an IDE
  • A docking system is the most work to build
Open A

B Workspaces

like Blender's workspaces, Fusion 360

Five fixed layouts, one per stage: Model, Render, Optics, Analyse, Report. Each shows only what its stage needs; the Analyse one has the tuning sliders beside the charts.

  • Learnable in minutes: the tabs are the process
  • Every layout is designed, never messy
  • Cheap: fixed grids, no docking
  • Switching stages to change one thing
  • A layout that does not fit a task cannot be changed
Open B

C Study

like a design-of-experiments tool, Grasshopper's Galapagos

The loop is the program. A right column holds the goal, the parameters (sent there from any property with ◇), the scores and every run. The view and the scored map share the centre.

  • The uniformity case on one screen, with history
  • The step to sweep and optimise is already there
  • Runs become the report
  • Rendering a picture is a side show
  • Too much for a quick look at a scene
Open C

D Canvas

like Figma, Spline, modern 3D web apps

The view is the whole window and everything floats over it. Selecting a thing shows its analysis: a detector shows its map, a light its polar diagram. Numbers can be pinned to objects in the view. Everything else is in a command palette.

  • The most picture, the least chrome
  • Analysis is attached to what it measures
  • A palette finds any of the ~150 settings by name
  • Floating panels cover the view they float on
  • Comparing two things means popping charts out
Open D

A recommendation, for you to overrule

They combine better than they compete. The one I would build:

  1. A's shell: a menu bar, split panes holding tab stacks, a status bar. Every panel is labelled with the module it belongs to. Tabs can be dragged between stacks; a fully free docking system can come later, if it is ever missed.
  2. B's workspaces as saved layouts of that shell: Model, Optics, Analyse, Render. A new user gets a designed layout per stage; someone who rearranges a layout keeps it.
  3. C's study as a panel (parameters, scores, runs), docked in Analyse by default. It is the one new thing the loop needs, and it holds the sweep and the optimiser later.
  4. D's ideas where they are cheap: a command palette over everything, and an inspector that shows the selected detector's or light's numbers.
  5. Charts carry their own controls, in a strip on top: planes, log, range, units, pin as reference, export. The Optics panel keeps only what changes the data.
