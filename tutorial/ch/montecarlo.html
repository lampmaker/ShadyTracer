Part IV · Path tracing · Chapter 12

Dice and averages

The light reaching one pixel comes from every direction, bounced off everything in the scene. Nobody can add all of that up exactly. A path tracer guesses instead: it follows one random path, then another, and averages what they bring back. This chapter is about why an average of random guesses gets the right answer, how fast it gets there, how the renderer knows how far it still is, and what happens when the dice are loaded.

1An answer from random guesses

Throw darts at a square one unit on a side, anywhere at random. A quarter circle of radius one covers π/4 of the square, so that is the chance a dart lands inside it. Score 4 for a dart inside and 0 for one outside: the average score is then π. Twenty darts give 3.4. That is a Monte Carlo estimate: an answer worked out as the average of random tries.

Throw more (the button) and the estimate wanders less and settles towards π. The plot shows it after every few darts, on a scale where each step right is twice the darts, with an error bar either side: the faint lines. The bar says how far from the true answer the estimate typically is, and it is worked out from the darts themselves, without knowing π.

After 81,920 darts the estimate is 3.1440 ± 0.0057. The true value is 0.4 error bars away. An error bar is not a wall: about one estimate in three lands more than one bar away, one in twenty more than two. The readout counts the bars each time; Start again throws new darts.

A pixel is the same game. Each sample sends one random path through the pixel and brings back the light it found, the pixel's value is the average of what its samples brought back, and the noise in a young picture is each pixel's estimate still wandering.

2Four times the work, half the error

How fast does it settle? Here are 240 separate estimates of π for each number of darts N, each dot one estimate. With 4 darts they scatter over the whole range; with 16, half as far; with 64, half again. The solid bar beside each column is how far they spread, measured from the dots (their standard deviation); the dashed one is the prediction, the spread of one dart divided by √N. Four times the darts, half the spread: that rule holds for every Monte Carlo estimate, and it is why a noisy picture needs four times the samples to look twice as clean.

The mathsWhy √N

Each dart scores X = 4 with probability p = π/4, else 0. Its average is 4p = π, and its spread, the standard deviation σ, is 4√(p(1 − p)) = 1.642. The average of N independent darts has the same middle, and a spread of

σmean = σ√N

σ
how far one try typically lands from the answer
N
how many tries are averaged

The error bar in section 1 is this, with σ worked out from the darts so far: σmean ≈ s/√N, where s² is the spread of the scores about their own average. It is called the standard error. Nothing in it depends on how many dimensions the problem has, which is why Monte Carlo is the way to add up light arriving from every direction.

4Loaded dice

    
All of this assumes the random numbers are fair: every value between 0 and 1 equally likely, every small stretch
      of [0, 1) hit as often as its width says. A GPU has no dice; it has a pseudo-random number
      generator, a formula that turns one number into the next in a way that looks random. ShadyTracer's is PCG4D
      (section 5). Its old one was fract(f32(state) / 671040), and the comment above the new one says why it
      went.

    
Here is the share of all possible states that land in each of 24 small windows of [0, 1),
      worked out exactly, for the old formula (left bars) and for PCG4D's output (right). The line at 1 is the fair
      share. In windows 0.001 wide, the old one is within about 4% of fair.

    
In windows of 0.0001 it is not: some get 0.95 of their share and some 1.83. The reason:
      f32(state) keeps only the top 24 of the state's 32 bits, so seven-eighths of all states become
      multiples of 64 or more, and after the division those land only on a grid of points 1/10,485 apart. A window
      0.0001 wide holds one of those points or two.

    
In windows of 0.00001, narrower than the grid, some get almost nothing (0.01 of their
      share) and some nearly nine times. PCG4D's output, the top 24 bits of its state, is a grid 1/16,777,216 apart with
      every point equally likely: fair down to windows far smaller than these.

    
Why would a window matter? A small, bright sun is a small window: of all the random directions a bounce can
      take, the few that hit the sun. With loaded dice, the chance of hitting it depends on where its window falls on the
      grid, which is different for every pixel. No number of samples averages that away: each pixel settled,
      confidently, on its own wrong answer.

6Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
An error bar from a count. With aiming at the light switched off, a 5° sun is found only when a
        bounce happens to go into it: about 50,000 times in 37 million samples (1188 × 969 pixels, 32 samples each, on the
        user's Pascal card, docs/TESTING.md, "BSDF parity"). The document puts the standard error near
        0.45%; 1/√50,000 is 0.45%. Light that arrives through rare events is as noisy as the count of those events.

    

    

      
A true mean, not a window. The average can forget old samples (a "window" of the last few),
        which keeps a moving picture fresh. It used to, by default: a window of 10, worth about 21 samples. A still
        picture then never converged; under the tone curve nobody saw it, and in the false-colour view every pixel kept
        flickering between bands. Moving the camera restarts the picture anyway, so the default is now the true mean
        ().

    

    

      
No sum of squares. The textbook variance, the mean of the squares less the square of the mean,
        subtracts two nearly equal numbers wherever the noise is small beside the light, and in 32-bit numbers that
        leaves nothing. The update in section 3 never forms either sum.

    

  






  

    
7What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • Loaded dice: the old generator, section 4.
    • 
      
  • A light that leaked into the next sample. resolve used to stop at once for a pixel
        no path landed on this sample, before clearing that pixel's slot, so light a shadow ray had added there was
        counted in the next sample instead. It now takes and clears every slot first (the comments in
        prog_resolve).
    • 
      
  • 4K pictures that would not run. resolve once numbered pixels in one long row of
        workgroups, and a GPU allows at most 65,535 workgroups along one direction: 4,194,240 pixels. A 2048 × 2048
        picture was over by one workgroup. It is now one row of the picture per row of workgroups.
    • 
      
  • A window nobody needed: the averaging window of 10, section 6.
