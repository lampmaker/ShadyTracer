Part IV · Path tracing · Chapter 12

Dice and averages

The light reaching one pixel comes from every direction, bounced off everything in the scene. Nobody can add all of that up exactly. A path tracer guesses instead: it follows one random path, then another, and averages what they bring back. This chapter is about why an average of random guesses gets the right answer, how fast it gets there, how the renderer knows how far it still is, and what happens when the dice are loaded.

1An answer from random guesses

Throw darts at a square one unit on a side, anywhere at random. A quarter circle of radius one covers π/4 of the square, so that is the chance a dart lands inside it. Score 4 for a dart inside and 0 for one outside: the average score is then π. Twenty darts give 3.4. That is a Monte Carlo estimate: an answer worked out as the average of random tries.

Throw more (the button) and the estimate wanders less and settles towards π. The plot shows it after every few darts, on a log scale (each label along the bottom four or sixteen times the one before, so the first darts get as much room as the last thousands), with an error bar either side: the faint lines. The bar says how far from the true answer the estimate typically is, and it is worked out from the darts themselves, without knowing π. At the very start it cannot be trusted: the first twelve darts here all landed inside, they agree perfectly, and the bar says ±0 while the estimate, 4, is far from π. A bar worked out from the tries is only as good as the tries, and it settles after a few dozen.

After 81,920 darts the estimate is 3.1440 ± 0.0057. The true value is 0.4 error bars away. An error bar is not a wall: about one estimate in three lands more than one bar away, one in twenty more than two. The readout counts the bars each time; Start again throws new darts. And the bar only measures how much the tries disagree with each other. Dice loaded the same way every time (section 4) make tries that agree on a wrong answer, and the bar shrinks round it just the same: it can see noise, never bias.

A pixel is the same game. Each sample sends one random path through the pixel and brings back the light it found, the pixel's value is the average of what its samples brought back, and the noise in a young picture is each pixel's estimate still wandering.

2Four times the work, half the error

How fast does it settle? Here are 240 separate estimates of π for each number of darts N, each dot one estimate. With 4 darts they scatter over the whole range, and sit only on 1, 2, 3 or 4 (each dart adds 0 or 1 to the estimate); with 16, half as far; with 64, half again. The solid bar beside each column is how far they spread, measured from the dots (their standard deviation); the dashed one is the prediction, the spread of one dart divided by √N. Four times the darts, half the spread: that rule holds for every Monte Carlo estimate, and it is why a noisy picture needs four times the samples to look twice as clean. The measured spread is itself worked out from 240 dots, so it wanders too, by about 5% (New darts shows it): at N = 256 it may read 0.093 against the predicted 0.103. With 960 dots a column it wanders half as much: the same √N rule, applied to the spread itself. Point at a column to read its numbers.

The mathsWhy √N

Each dart scores X = 4 with probability p = π/4, else 0, so its average is 4p = π. How far it lands from that average, squared and averaged, is its variance: a score of 4 is 4 − π away, a score of 0 is π away, and

σ² = p(4 − π)² + (1 − p) π² = 16 p(1 − p)

(put π = 4p in and it simplifies). Its square root, the standard deviation, is σ = 4√(p(1 − p)) = 1.642. Now the √N. For tries that do not depend on each other, variances add: the sum of N darts has variance Nσ², so it spreads by √N σ. The average is that sum divided by N, and so is its spread:

σmean = √N σN = σ√N

σ
how far one try typically lands from the answer
N
how many tries are averaged

The error bar in section 1 is this, with σ worked out from the darts so far: σmean ≈ s/√N, where s is the spread of the scores about their own average. It is called the standard error. Nothing in it depends on how many random numbers each try is made of. A dart is two; a path through a scene is dozens (a direction at every bounce, a point of a lamp, a point of the lens). Spacing tries evenly instead, ten values for each of a dozen numbers, would take 10¹² tries; random tries settle as 1/√N however many numbers each one draws. That is why Monte Carlo is the way to add up light arriving from every direction.

3An error bar for every pixel

After each sample, a kernel (a program the GPU runs once for every pixel) called resolve adds the sample to each pixel's average. It keeps no sum. It moves the average towards the new sample by a share w = 1/cnt of the way, where cnt is how many samples the pixel now holds: the first sample is the average (w = 1), the second moves it halfway, the third a third of the way, and the result is always the plain average of all of them. With values 2, 4 and 9: 2, then 3, then 3 + (9 − 3)/3 = 5, which is (2 + 4 + 9)/3.

Beside the average it keeps two more numbers per pixel, so that the renderer knows each pixel's error bar too: the cursor readout in ShadyTracer shows it, and the test bench reads pictures by it. V is how much the pixel's samples scatter about their average (the average squared distance from it; for 2, 4 and 9, 8.67). S is the sum of the squares of the weights each sample has in the average. In a true average of n samples each weighs 1/n, so S = n · (1/n)² = 1/n, and the error bar the code works out, √(S · V / (1 − S)), becomes √(V / (n − 1)): the darts' s/√N exactly, with s worked out the usual way (dividing by n − 1, which corrects for measuring the scatter about the samples' own average rather than the true answer). For 2, 4 and 9 that is √(8.67/2) = 2.08. S is kept, rather than worked out from n, because the average can also forget old samples (section 6), and then the weights are not equal.

4Loaded dice

    
All of this assumes the random numbers are fair: every value between 0 and 1 equally likely, every small stretch
      of [0, 1) hit as often as its width says. A GPU has no dice; it has a pseudo-random number
      generator, a formula that turns one number into the next in a way that looks random. ShadyTracer's is PCG4D
      (section 5). Its old one was fract(f32(state) / 671040), and the comment above the new one says why it
      went.

    
Here is the share of all possible states that land in each of 24 small windows of [0, 1),
      worked out exactly, for the old formula (left bars) and for PCG4D's output (right). The line at 1 is the fair
      share. In windows 0.001 wide, the old one is within about 4% of fair.

    
In windows of 0.0001 it is not: some get 0.95 of their share and some 1.83. The reason
      starts with the state, the number the generator keeps and steps on: a whole number from 0 to about 4.3
      billion (2³², 32 binary digits). The old formula divided it by 671,040 and kept what is after the point
      (fract), a number in [0, 1): from 0 up to but not including 1. But first it turned the state into the
      GPU's usual kind of number, an f32, which keeps 24 significant binary digits, the way a
      calculator that shows seven digits turns 12,345,678 into 12,345,680. Every state below 2²⁴ (about 16.8 million)
      fits whole. Larger ones are rounded to the nearest multiple of 2, 4, 8 …, and every state from 2²⁹ up, which is
      seven-eighths of all of them, becomes a multiple of 64 or more. Divided by 671,040 (which is 64 × 10,485), those
      land only on a grid of points 1/10,485 apart. A window 0.0001 wide holds one of those points or two.

    
In windows of 0.00001, narrower than the grid, some get almost nothing (0.01 of their
      share) and some nearly nine times. PCG4D's output, the top 24 bits of its state divided by 2²⁴, is a grid
      1/16,777,216 apart with every point equally likely. A window of 0.00001 holds 167 or 168 of those points, so its
      share is within half a percent of fair (the readout's 0.995×), and the wider the window, the closer.

    
Why would a window matter? A small, bright sun is a small window: of all the random numbers a bounce can draw,
      the few that send it into the sun. Which numbers those are depends on the surface the bounce leaves (the numbers
      are turned into a direction about that surface's own normal), so, in the comment's words, "each pixel sees it at a
      different place on the grid": one pixel's window holds an extra grid point, its neighbour's misses one. No number
      of samples averages that away: each pixel settled, confidently, on its own wrong answer, and its error bar shrank
      round it.

6Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
An error bar from a count. With aiming at the light (next-event
        estimation, chapter 14) switched off, a sun 5° across is found only when a bounce happens to go into it:
        about 50,000 times in 37 million paths (1188 × 969 pixels, 32 samples each, on the user's graphics card, an
        NVIDIA Pascal; docs/TESTING.md, the section "BSDF parity"). The document puts the standard error near
        0.45%; 1/√50,000 is 0.45%. Light that arrives through rare events is as noisy as the count of those events.

    

    

      
A true mean, not a window. The average can forget old samples, which keeps a moving picture
        fresh: cnt stops growing at the window's size plus one, so each new sample keeps a fixed weight and the
        old ones fade. It used to, by default: a window of 10, each new sample weighing 1/11, which holds as much as a
        true average of about 21 samples (the code's comment: S settles near 1/(2N + 1)). A still
        picture then never converged. Through the usual tone curve (chapter 7) nobody saw it; in the false-colour view,
        which paints each band of brightness its own colour, every pixel kept flickering between bands. Moving the camera
        restarts the picture anyway, so the default is now the true mean
        ().

    

    

      
No sum of squares. The textbook variance, the mean of the squares less the square of the mean,
        subtracts two nearly equal numbers wherever the noise is small beside the light, and in 32-bit numbers that
        leaves nothing. The update in section 3 never forms either sum.

    

  






  

    
7What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • Loaded dice: the old generator, section 4.
    • 
      
  • A light that leaked into the next sample. Each pixel has a slot where a sample's light is
        added up before resolve takes it. resolve used to stop at once for a pixel no path
        landed on this sample, before clearing its slot, so light a shadow ray had added there
        was counted in the next sample instead. It now takes and clears every slot first (the comments in
        prog_resolve).
    • 
      
  • Large pictures that would not run. The GPU runs a kernel in groups of threads, here 64 pixels
        a group, and allows at most 65,535 groups along one direction. resolve once numbered pixels in one
        long row of groups, so it stopped at 64 × 65,535 = 4,194,240 pixels: a 2048 × 2048 picture was over by one group,
        and a 4K one far over. It is now one row of the picture per row of groups.
    • 
      
  • A window nobody needed: the averaging window of 10, section 6.
