Part VII · Speed · Chapter 25

Measure first

Every chapter of this part is about making ShadyTracer faster, and this one is about what has to come before that: knowing where the time goes, and knowing how sure a number is. It is the chapter of one rule, written into the repository's instructions after a plan built on a guess had to be undone, and of the tools that came out of it: timestamps on every kernel, a benchmark that changes one thing a row, and the habit of putting the version and the card beside every number.

1A guess written as a fact

One scene, the Noise library (an example scene of rows of shapes, each covered in a different noise relief: marble, granite, leather), rendered at well under two samples a second. A sample is one noisy try at the whole picture, a path of light traced through every pixel; the picture on the screen is the average of many (chapters 12 and 23), so the time one sample takes is how fast the renderer is. A plan to speed ShadyTracer up called this scene's time "this architecture's full-resolution cost", and set out to change the architecture. Here is that time in the benchmark's first full run, on the user's GTX 1080 Ti (an NVIDIA card of the generation called Pascal, which is what the documents call it): 736.5 ms a sample at 1280 × 720.

The same card, the same size, the same code, the other bundled scenes: 61.8 to 177.2 ms, the demo 7.7 times faster. The user had said as much, "about 6x faster at the same resolution", before the plan was written. A cost of the architecture would be paid by every scene. One scene paying it is a fact about that scene.

And the scene's own rows, each changing one thing, say which. Without its six reliefs the Noise library renders in 81.1 ms, beside Surface materials, the same layout without them, at 80.0. The rows marked "only relief" keep one of the six, named by how high its bumps reach and how far apart they are (1 mm high, features about 10 mm across): each alone adds 72 to 91 ms. They do not simply add up: alone, the six add 484 ms summed, and together 655 ms, a difference the run's notes record and do not explain. Either way the time was the reliefs: the noise they evaluate at every step near a surface (chapter 26 is what was done about it). The plan was reverted, and the repository's instructions gained the rule this tutorial stamps every number with:


  

  





  

    
2Where the time goes

    
A sample's time on the card (the GPU) is spent in its
      kernels: small programs, each run over thousands of rays at once. A page reaches the card
      through WebGPU, the browser's way of handing it work, and WebGPU takes work in compute passes; ShadyTracer
      gives every run of a kernel a pass of its own, about 200 in this sample. They are chapter 22's: genrays
      makes the camera's rays; then, round after round (an iteration), march moves every ray up to
      32 steps, surface and shade work out what each ray found and where it goes next,
      occlude traces the shadow rays, and four small kernels (three prep_ ones and
      flip) only size the next kernel's work or swap its queues; resolve, last, adds the sample
      into the picture. The sample is chapter 22's room, a small room in the flat world (this tutorial's
      two-dimensional scenes) lit by the sun through a window, in 24 iterations.

    
To time the passes, ShadyTracer has the card write a timestamp,
      its own clock in nanoseconds, as each pass starts and as it ends, and reads them all back after the sample. Each
      row here is a kernel and each mark a pass at its timestamps; one march pass is marked with its two, in
      nanoseconds from the first. The times are a model's, made from how many rays and steps each pass handled in the
      flat world (the caption says how); what reads them is the engine's own code, kerneltime.js, run in
      this page. Point at a pass.

    
Added up by kernel (tally): the march takes half of this sample, shade
      a fifth, occlusion a seventh, the surface kernel a ninth. Each of the four small kernels runs 24 times, once an
      iteration, and its 24 passes together take about a tenth of a millisecond. The part of the sample's span no pass covers is the gaps between passes, here 0.4 ms of 88: what
      launching one kernel after another costs. Wall clock, the time the program waits for the sample, is neither:
      it adds everything outside the card (chapter 22 measured that part at 1.5 ms plus 2.35 ms an iteration).

    
By iteration (perIteration, every pass from a
      prep_march to its flip): the first eleven carry most of the work, the twelfth about half
      as much (chapter 22 says why it drops there), and the drained tail (the last iterations, when only a few slow rays
      are left) costs a little each. Timing by kernel and by iteration is how the benchmark found the budget that
      shrank by accident (section 6): its rows ran 15 iterations where row 1 ran 24.

    
One catch. A browser may round the timestamps, to keep a page from timing things it
      should not (a fine enough clock lets a page measure what other code on the machine is doing). Chrome's
      documentation gives 100 µs; the user's fifth run, in Chrome 154 on the Ampere card (a later NVIDIA generation; the
      documents do not name the model), read every small kernel in multiples of 32.768 µs, and the run's notes record
      the difference without explaining it. Rounded to 100 µs, 124 of the 194 passes here read 0, and a few read 0.1
      ms, which no single pass took (marked above them). Added up, the big kernels' totals still come out near their
      true values (the march 44.6 ms against 44.3); the small ones' only on average, over more passes than one sample
      has: prep_march reads 0 in all where its passes took 0.1 ms, prep_occlude 0.2 where
      they took 0.1. The gaps move too, since they are the span less the passes, both read from rounded stamps: 0.20 ms
      here against 0.39.

    
Why on average: the start and the end are each rounded down to the tick before them,
      separately, so a pass reads the whole ticks between its two rounded stamps. A 4 µs pass reads 0 when no tick
      mark falls inside it and a whole tick, 100 µs, when one does. It holds a tick mark when its start lands in the
      4 µs just before one: 4 µs in every 100. Placed anywhere, then, it reads a tick 4 times in a hundred, and its
      reading averages 4% of 100 µs: its own 4 µs. (Ten thousand random placements give 4.33: an average of random
      draws wobbles a little round the truth.) Drag the pass across a tick mark, and change its length: a rounded
      timestamp is right on average over many passes and wrong for any one of them.

  

  





  

    
3The same code, another number

    
The same kernels (their text the same to the byte), the same scene and settings, on
      the user's GTX 1080 Ti in one evening: 184.7, 184.8, 216.6 and 255.5 ms a sample of GPU time. Nothing in the code
      differed. Two measures of how far apart they are. The range: how far the highest is above the lowest,
      38%. The spread: their standard deviation as a share of their mean, 16% (the readings' distances from
      their mean squared, added, divided by one less than the number of readings, and the square root taken: the usual
      way for a handful of readings). The card's own speed moves, with its heat and power state or other work on it (inferred: the
      readings cannot say which), and the review that collected these says a comparison between runs "is good to
      about ±20%, and sometimes not that".

    
So time a change in a run of its own, and the old code in another: the drift
      between the two runs lands in the difference. Here build B is truly 10% faster than A, and 40 comparisons are
      made under a model of that drift (each run's speed its own, spread by 16% as those four readings are): in 17 of
      them B reads no faster at all, and only 8 land within 5 points of the truth (a point: one percent, so between
      −5% and −15% here). A 10% change cannot be seen this way.

    
Interleave instead: A, B, A, B in one run, so both share the card's mood of the
      moment, and compare the mean of B's two rows with the mean of A's. Only the small noise between rows next to
      each other is left: about 1.5% in the model, from the documents' rows repeated straight after themselves (0.38%,
      and 1.3% in later runs). Rows far apart in one run drift further, as section 5's repeated row shows. All 40
      comparisons read B faster, 37 of them within 2 points of the truth. The benchmark repeats its first row in the
      middle and at the end for the same reason, a drift check, and can run its rows, by number, in any order:
      BENCH.run(33, 35, 33, 35).

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Timestamps cost nothing the benchmark can see. A pair of timestamps a pass is not free, the
        timer's header says; so the benchmark times kernels in samples of their own, after the wall-clock ones, and
        reports those samples' wall clock too. It came out within −7% to +6% of the untimed samples', some faster and
        some slower: inside the run's own drift (the user's Ampere card, run 5, docs/PERFORMANCE.md).

    

    

      
One row again, to see the drift. In the benchmark's second run the first row, repeated after
        the settings rows and after the unit rows, read 737.3, 784.2 and 714.9 ms: the highest 9.7% above the lowest
        (the notes put it as 9.3% of their mean). "Nothing here under ~10% is resolved", they conclude, and the "slower when doing less" of the first run (rows that do less work, such as fewer bounces, reading 6%
        slower than row 1) was drift
        (the user's GTX 1080 Ti, docs/PERFORMANCE.md).

    

    

      
A median, and the median of totals. A row's time is the median of its samples, not their
        mean, so one sample stalled by something else on the card moves it little; and a row's GPU time is the median of
        each sample's total, not the sum of each kernel's median, which no single sample need have taken
        (tests.js kernelSummary; kerneltime.test.mjs holds it).

    

    

      
Warm up, then time, then time the kernels. At least 3 samples and 2 seconds thrown away
        (glowing objects being found, the first frame's timing, the photon map's first build); then at least 4 samples
        and 5 seconds, at most 64; then the kernel-timed samples, at least 4 and a second (bench/index.js,
        BENCH).

    

    

      
Why the card drifts. Heat, power state, or other work on the card: inferred in the review,
        not measured; the readings above show only that it does, by up to 38% in an evening.

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • The plan. Section 1, as it happened: it is the reason for the rule, and this tutorial's
        stamps.
    • 
      
  • The wrong scene. The benchmark was first built around Surface materials, the scene the user
        had named as the slow one; then: "I mentioned the surface earlier as the hardest scene, but I meant noise"
        ().
        A number is only as good as knowing which scene made it.
    • 
      
  • A camera inside a lamp. The benchmark's cameras took elevation as degrees above
        the horizon, where the camera code means degrees off vertical: the first runs looked down through the scene's
        softbox, and measured that ().
        The user saw it; no number showed it.
    • 
      
  • A budget that shrank between rows. A sample's budget is how many iterations it is
        given. A ray may take maxiter steps in all, 400 by default, at most 32 an iteration, and a path at
        most 10 bounces, so 11 legs: the worst case is 11 + 400/32 rounded up, 24 iterations. The budget was adaptive then: never above that
        worst case, and raised again only when more than 1% of rays were cut short, still marching when the iterations
        ran out. The per-kernel pass counts showed rows 6 on running 15 iterations where rows 1 to 5 ran 24. The
        "maxiter 128" row (128 steps a ray, not 400) had lowered the worst case to 11 + 4, and the budget with it; nothing reset it between rows, too few rays were cut short to raise it, and it stayed there,
        so every later row was compared with a row 1 that did more work
        (;
        chapter 22's last story is what else the same budget did by accident, and why it is now always the worst
        case).
    • 
      
  • A frame timed with its wait. The time a frame took was measured from its recording to its
        end, which with a frame queued behind another includes the wait: a 200 ms sample read about 400
        ().
    • 
      
  • A compile time read from the browser's cache. A profile of each kernel's compile first read
        shade at 12 ms: it compiled the same text the page already had, and the browser handed back its cached result
        ().
