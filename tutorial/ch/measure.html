Part VII · Speed · Chapter 25

Measure first

Every chapter of this part is about making ShadyTracer faster, and this one is about what has to come before that: knowing where the time goes, and knowing how sure a number is. It is the chapter of one rule, written into the repository's instructions after a plan built on a guess had to be undone, and of the tools that came out of it: timestamps on every kernel, a benchmark that changes one thing a row, and the habit of putting the version and the card beside every number.

1A guess written as a fact

One scene, the Noise library (an example scene of rows of shapes, each covered in a different noise relief: marble, granite, leather), rendered at well under two samples a second. A plan to speed ShadyTracer up called this "this architecture's full-resolution cost", and set out to change the architecture. Here is the scene's time in the benchmark's first full run, on the user's GTX 1080 Ti: 736.5 ms a sample at 1280 × 720.

The same card, the same size, the same code, the other bundled scenes: 61.8 to 177.2 ms, the demo 7.7 times faster. The user had said as much, "about 6x faster at the same resolution", before the plan was written. A cost of the architecture would be paid by every scene. One scene paying it is a fact about that scene.

And the scene's own rows, each changing one thing, say which. Without its six reliefs the Noise library renders in 81.1 ms, beside Surface materials, the same layout without them, at 80.0. Each relief alone adds 72 to 91 ms. The time was the reliefs: the noise they evaluate at every step near a surface (chapter 26 is what was done about it). The plan was reverted, and the repository's instructions gained the rule this tutorial stamps every number with:


  

  





  

    
2Where the time goes

    
A sample's time on the card is spent in its kernels, one
      compute pass each time one runs: about 200 of them in this sample (chapter 22's room, eight kernels an
      iteration, 24 iterations). To time them, ShadyTracer has the card write a timestamp, the card's own
      clock in nanoseconds, as each pass starts and as it ends, and reads them all back after the sample. Each row here
      is a kernel and each mark a pass at its timestamps. The times are a model's, made from the flat world's counts
      (the caption says how); what reads them is the engine's own code, kerneltime.js, run in this page.
      Point at a pass.

    
Added up by kernel (tally): the march takes half of this sample, shade
      a fifth, occlusion a seventh, the surface kernel a ninth, and the small kernels between them (the three prep
      kernels, the flip) a tenth of a millisecond each over 24 passes. The part of the sample's span no pass covers is the gaps between passes, here 0.4 ms of 88: what
      launching one kernel after another costs. Wall clock, the time the program waits for the sample, is neither:
      it adds everything outside the card (chapter 22 measured that part at 1.5 ms plus 2.35 ms an iteration).

    
By iteration (perIteration, every pass from a
      prep_march to its flip): the first eleven carry the work, and the drained tail of
      chapter 22 costs a little each. Timing by kernel and by iteration is how the benchmark found the budget that
      shrank by accident (section 6): its rows ran 15 iterations where row 1 ran 24.

    
One catch. A browser may round the timestamps, to keep pages from timing things
      they should not: Chrome documents 100 µs, and the user's run read multiples of 32.768 µs. Rounded to 100 µs, 124 of the 194
      passes read 0 and a few read 0.1 ms, which no single pass took. Added up, the big kernels' totals still come
      out near their true values (the march 44.6 ms against 44.3); the small ones' only on average, over more passes
      than one sample has: prep_march reads 0 where it took 0.1 ms, prep_occlude 0.2. A
      rounded timestamp is right on average over many passes and wrong for any one of them.

  

  





  

    
3The same code, another number

    
The same kernels (their text the same to the byte), the same scene and settings, on
      the user's Pascal card in one evening: 184.7, 184.8, 216.6 and 255.5 ms a sample of GPU time. Nothing in the code
      differed. The card's own speed moves, with its heat and power state or other work on it (inferred: the readings
      cannot say which), and the review that collected these says a comparison between runs "is good to about ±20%,
      and sometimes not that".

    
So time a change in a run of its own, and the old code in another: the drift
      between the two runs lands in the difference. Here build B is truly 10% faster than A, and 40 comparisons are
      made under a model of that drift (each run's speed its own, spread as those four readings are): in 17 of them B
      reads no faster at all, and only 8 land within 5 points of the truth. A 10% change cannot be seen this way.

    
Interleave instead: A, B, A, B in one run, so both share the card's mood of the
      moment, and only the small noise between rows next to each other is left. All 40 comparisons read B faster, 37 of
      them within 2 points of the truth. The benchmark repeats its first row in the middle and at the end for the same reason,
      a drift check, and can run rows in any order: BENCH.run(33, 35, 33, 35).

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Timestamps cost nothing visible. The benchmark times kernels in samples of their own, after
        the wall-clock ones, and reports those samples' wall clock too: within −7% to +6% of the untimed samples', both
        ways, and every small kernel a multiple of 0.032768 ms (the user's Ampere card, run 5,
        docs/PERFORMANCE.md).

    

    

      
One row again, to see the drift. In the benchmark's second run the first row, repeated after
        the settings rows and after the unit rows, read 737.3, 784.2 and 714.9 ms: 9.3% of their mean. "Nothing here
        under ~10% is resolved", the run's notes conclude, and the "slower when doing less" of the first run was drift
        (the user's GTX 1080 Ti, docs/PERFORMANCE.md).

    

    

      
A median, and the median of totals. A row's time is the median of its samples, not their
        mean, so one sample stalled by something else on the card moves it little; and a row's GPU time is the median of
        each sample's total, not the sum of each kernel's median, which no single sample need have taken
        (tests.js kernelSummary; kerneltime.test.mjs holds it).

    

    

      
Warm up, then time, then time the kernels. At least 3 samples and 2 seconds thrown away
        (glowing objects being found, the first frame's timing, the photon map's first build); then at least 4 samples
        and 5 seconds, at most 64; then the kernel-timed samples, at least 4 and a second (bench/index.js,
        BENCH).

    

    

      
Why the card drifts. Heat, power state, or other work on the card: inferred in the review,
        not measured; the readings above show only that it does, by up to 38% in an evening.

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • The plan. Section 1, as it happened: it is the reason for the rule, and this tutorial's
        stamps.
    • 
      
  • The wrong scene. The benchmark was first built around Surface materials, the scene the user
        had named as the slow one; then: "I mentioned the surface earlier as the hardest scene, but I meant noise"
        ().
        A number is only as good as knowing which scene made it.
    • 
      
  • A camera inside a lamp. The benchmark's cameras took elevation as degrees above
        the horizon, where the camera code means degrees off vertical: the first runs looked down through the scene's
        softbox, and measured that ().
        The user saw it; no number showed it.
    • 
      
  • A budget that shrank between rows. The per-kernel pass counts showed rows 6 on running 15
        iterations where rows 1 to 5 ran 24: the adaptive budget, never reset between rows, had been pulled down by the
        "maxiter 128" row and stayed there, so every later row was compared with a row 1 that did more work
        (;
        chapter 22's last story).
    • 
      
  • A frame timed with its wait. The time a frame took was measured from its recording to its
        end, which with a frame queued behind another includes the wait: a 200 ms sample read about 400
        ().
    • 
      
  • A compile time read from the browser's cache. A profile of each kernel's compile first read
        shade at 12 ms: it compiled the same text the page already had, and the browser handed back its cached result
        ().
