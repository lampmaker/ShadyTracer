The camera's lens

Chapter 1's camera is a pinhole: every ray starts at one point, and everything is sharp at every distance. A real camera has a lens, an opening with a width, and only things at one distance are sharp. Giving the renderer's camera a lens takes a few lines, and it changes what the camera can and cannot see in a way that matters for more than pictures.

1A lens is an opening with a focus

ShadyTracer's lens is a thin lens: a disc of radius R round the eye (here, seen from the side, the thick bar; the eye drawn just behind it marks where the pinhole was), focused at a distance F (the red dashed line, the focus plane; drag it by its foot). The rule is one sentence: each pixel looks at one point of the focus plane, and all its rays aim there, each from a random point of the lens. That point is where the pinhole's ray for the pixel crosses the focus plane. So a point on the focus plane is reached from every point of the lens by the rays of one pixel: the rays drawn from across the lens all meet there, and the picture (the column on the right) shows it as one dot. The column is drawn there for reading, not where a film would be: the renderer has no film, a pixel is a direction, and the column shows which pixel each ray belongs to, top at the top.

Drag the point nearer. Now the rays from across the lens that pass through it are the rays of different pixels: carried on (dashed), they cross the focus plane at different heights, and each height is another pixel's point. On the picture the point spreads into a streak, its blur (in 3D a disc, the circle of confusion). Its size is 2R |1/F − 1/D|: here 2 × 0.6 × |1/6 − 1/3.2| = 0.175, measured on a picture one unit in front of the eye. The picture's half-height there is tan(fov/2), half the field of view's spread at one unit, which for this camera's 40° is 0.364; so the blur is 0.175 / 0.364 = 0.48 of the half-height, about 240 pixels of a picture 1000 high. Larger lens, larger blur; nearer the focus, smaller.

Shrink the lens to nothing (R = 0) and every ray starts at the eye: the pinhole, sharp everywhere. In ShadyTracer a radius of 0 is the pinhole exactly, the same ray from the same random numbers, so a picture without a lens is the one it always was.

The mathsThe blur

A ray from the lens point at height a to the point at distance D and height h rises h − a over a distance D (similar triangles), so at the focus plane, a distance F out, it is at height a + (h − a) F/D. That point belongs to the pixel whose pinhole ray passes through it, from the eye at height 0: that ray rises its height over F, so divide by F to get the picture position, on a picture one unit in front of the eye:

y = hD + a (1F − 1D)

Over the lens, a runs from −R to R, so the blur is 2R |1/F − 1/D|: zero at the focus, growing with the lens, and growing with the difference between 1/F and 1/D (so a point twice as far as the focus blurs less than one half as far: |1/F − 1/2F| is half of |1/F − 2/F|). Divide by tan(fov/2), the picture's half-height at one unit, for the share of the half-height. In 3D the streak is a disc, the circle of confusion.

2Depth of field

The same in 3D, a picture made the way the renderer makes it. A real lens gathers light through every point of its opening at once, and a pixel shows the average over all of them. The renderer cannot trace them all, so each sample takes one random point of the lens for every pixel (chapter 12): averaged over samples, the picture becomes that average, and the blur appears as samples add up, grainy at first. Three balls stand at three distances: red near, blue in the middle, gold far. With no aperture, a pinhole, all three are sharp; 4 samples a pixel are enough, since there is nothing to average over the lens.

With an aperture of 0.25 (the lens's diameter, twice the radius R of the first figure) focused on the middle ball, the near and the far one blur, each by its own 1/D from the focus. This is depth of field: the range of distances that look sharp.

Focused on the near ball, it sharpens and the others go soft.

A wider aperture, 0.6, shrinks the range that looks sharp: the middle and the far ball are much softer now. Each picture takes a few dozen samples a pixel to lose its grain (48 here): the blur is itself an average over the lens.

3How ShadyTracer does it


    
Why a lens, beyond depth of field: a path from a lamp, into glass, onto a floor, back out through the glass to the
      eye is fixed at every step but one. Glass bends (refracts) light by a rule, so each turn through it is decided; a
      matte (diffuse) floor scatters light every way, so the path is free there, and only there. With a point for an
      eye, the last refraction must send the light at exactly that point, and a free choice at the floor has no chance
      of hitting a single point. A lens is an area, which a choice can hit. The lens's own file says so:

    
The camera ray, in the kernel that makes them, piece by piece: right, up and fwd are the
      camera's three directions (fwd points backwards, out of the scene, so −fwd looks into it). The bracket is the
      pinhole's ray before it is made one unit long: it goes exactly one unit forward. Times F
      (UB.camLens.y; UB holds the values handed to the GPU) it reaches the focus plane.
      Less off, the lens point's offset from the eye, it is the way from the lens point to that point of
      the focus plane; normalize makes it one unit long.

    

    
The camera hands the kernels half its aperture (the radius) and its focus, or the distance to what it looks at
      when no focus is set, so a view you turn round its target stays focused on it

4Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means. The first is for readers who have
      read chapters 18 and 19.

    

      
The lens's area cancels. When light found by tracing from a lamp is connected to the camera, the
        connection picks a point on the lens, and the pixel's value is an average over the lens: one divides by the
        lens's area, the other's chance of picking that point carries the same area, and the two cancel. So the
        connection's weight is the pinhole's, measured from the lens point instead of the eye (lens.js, "A
        pixel's value, written as a measurement").

    

    

      
No lens, no change. The lens's two random numbers are drawn whether there is a lens or not, so
        turning the lens on or off changes nothing else in the sequence of random numbers a picture uses.

    

  






  

    
5What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A black floor in a prism. With a pinhole, a floor inside a prism lit by a lamp through the
        prism looked black through the prism: light → glass → floor → glass → eye has no direction left to choose at the
        end, so a point camera can never be found by it (the user's picture, 2026-10-08). The lens, an area, was added in
        version 0.43.0 partly for that.
    • 
      
  • White specks. From 0.43.0 photons that happened to pass through the lens added their light to
        the pixel they crossed. That counted the floor behind glass only when a photon happened through: white specks in
        the user's picture of 2026-10-15. The photon map (chapter 19) took that light over in 0.61.0, through any
        aperture or none, and the lens kept only its depth of field.
    • 
      
  • Focal length and aperture that did nothing. Depth-of-field settings once existed that no part
        of the renderer used; they were removed in version 0.22.6, and the lens came back, working, as aperture and focus
        in 0.43.0 (the comment in lib/camera.js).
