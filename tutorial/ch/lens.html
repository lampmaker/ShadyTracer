The camera's lens

Chapter 1's camera is a pinhole: every ray starts at one point, and everything is sharp at every distance. A real camera has a lens, an opening with a width, and only things at one distance are sharp. Giving the renderer's camera a lens takes a few lines, and it changes what the camera can and cannot see in a way that matters for more than pictures.

1A lens is an opening with a focus

ShadyTracer's lens is a thin lens: a disc of radius R round the eye (here, seen from the side, the thick bar), focused at a distance F (the dashed line). To make a pixel's ray, start from a random point of the disc and aim at the point where the pinhole's ray for that pixel crosses the focus plane. A point on the focus plane is reached from every point of the lens by the rays of one pixel: the rays drawn from across the lens all meet there, and the picture (the column on the right) shows it as one dot.

Drag the point nearer. Now the rays from across the lens that pass through it are the rays of different pixels: carried on, they cross the focus plane at different heights. On the picture the point spreads into a streak, its blur. Its size is 2R |1/F − 1/D| (over the picture's half-height): here 0.48, about 240 pixels of a picture 1000 high. Larger lens, larger blur; nearer the focus, smaller.

Shrink the lens to nothing (R = 0) and every ray starts at the eye: the pinhole, sharp everywhere. In ShadyTracer a radius of 0 is the pinhole exactly, the same ray, and draws no extra random number, so a picture without a lens is the one it always was.

The mathsThe blur

From a lens point at height a, a point at distance D and height h is seen in the direction that crosses the focus plane at height a + (h − a) F/D, which through the pinhole is the picture position

y = hD + a (1F − 1D)

(in units of the distance to a picture one unit away). Over the lens, a runs from −R to R, so the blur is 2R |1/F − 1/D|: zero at the focus, growing with the lens and with the distance from the focus measured in 1/D. In 3D the streak is a disc, the circle of confusion.

2Depth of field

The same in 3D, a picture made the way the renderer makes it: each pixel averages many rays, each from a random point of the lens. Three balls stand at three distances. With no aperture, a pinhole, all three are sharp.

With an aperture of 0.25 (the lens's diameter) focused on the middle ball, the near and the far one blur, each by its own 1/D from the focus. This is depth of field: the range of distances that look sharp.

Focused on the near ball, it sharpens and the others go soft.

A wider aperture, 0.6, shrinks the range that looks sharp: the far balls are much softer now. Each picture takes a few dozen samples a pixel to lose its grain: the blur is itself an average over the lens.

The lens's area cancels. When light found by tracing from a lamp is connected to the camera, the
        connection picks a point on the lens, and the pixel's value is an average over the lens: one divides by the
        lens's area, the other's chance of picking that point carries the same area, and the two cancel. So the
        connection's weight is the pinhole's, measured from the lens point instead of the eye (lens.js, "A
        pixel's value, written as a measurement").

    

    

      
No lens, no change. A radius of 0 takes no random number and gives the pinhole's ray, so turning
        the lens on or off changes nothing else in a picture's random stream.

    

  






  

    
5What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A black floor in a prism. With a pinhole, a floor inside a prism lit by a lamp through the
        prism looked black through the prism: light → glass → floor → glass → eye has no direction left to choose at the
        end, so a point camera can never be found by it (the user's picture, 2026-10-08). The lens, an area, was added in
        version 0.43.0 partly for that.
    • 
      
  • White specks. From 0.43.0 photons that happened to pass through the lens added their light to
        the pixel they crossed. That counted the floor behind glass only when a photon happened through: white specks in
        the user's picture of 2026-10-15. The photon map (chapter 19) took that light over in 0.61.0, through any
        aperture or none, and the lens kept only its depth of field.
    • 
      
  • Focal length and aperture with no page. Depth-of-field controls once existed that nothing
        showed; they went in 0.22.6, and the lens came back as aperture and focus in 0.43.0 (the comment in
        lib/camera.js).
