Part V · Light the camera can't find · Chapter 18

Caustics

Sunlight through a glass of water makes a bright, wavy patch on the table under it: a caustic, light that glass or a mirror has gathered onto a surface. Everything so far follows light backwards from the eye, and for a caustic that fails: the eye's paths almost never find it. This chapter is why, and the cure ShadyTracer uses, following light forwards from the sun as well, with a rule that shares the work out so that no light is counted twice.

1A shadow where the light should be

A glass ball over a matte floor, the sun 75° up on the right, in the flat world (this tutorial's 2D world, where light spreads round a circle instead of over a sphere: chapter 0). The ball bends the sun's light together, so the floor under it should show a bright spot. The eye's picture is the strip below, made as chapter 14 makes it: a path from the eye through every pixel, and at each point of the floor a shadow ray aimed at the sun (next-event estimation, NEE). One path through every pixel is a sample; the strip is the average of the samples so far, and the readout's ± is its error bar: the average lies within about that of the true value two times in three (chapter 12). Under the ball (bracketed, pixels 22 to 29) the shadow rays meet the glass and count it as in the way: a shadow, with bright specks. The open floor reads 0.39 from the first sample; under the ball the average jumps about from sample to sample and settles only slowly.

The shadow ray cannot do better. It has to aim at the sun, but through the ball the sun is not where it is: the glass bends the line. The only line from the floor through the glass to the sun is the one Snell's law makes (the law of how glass bends light, chapter 10), and a shadow ray picked at random lies on it with chance 0. A mirror or glass sends light one exact way (chapter 8: a delta), so there is nothing to aim at.

What is left is the floor's bounce: its direction picked at random (chapter 8), it goes into the ball, and only if the glass happens to turn it onto the sun does it bring the sun's light back. In the flat world that happens about once in 220 bounces for a sun half a degree across. A find carries the sun's brightness, 106, times the 0.8 the floor sends back: 85, where the open floor reads 0.39. It counts that much because a path's weight is what it found divided by the chance of finding it (chapter 8): a rare find counts for a lot, and most samples find nothing. On average the sums agree: once in 220 bounces, 85 each, is 85 ÷ 220 ≈ 0.39, what the open floor gets; the glass moves the sun's light about, it does not add to it. Those rare big finds are the specks, and why the average jumps. In 3D the real sun is found far more rarely: 1 bounce in 66,111, for a sun 45° up (chapter 5's case).

Make the sun 8° across, the light it sends onto a surface facing it held the same (its illuminance, as ShadyTracer's setting holds it: chapter 5), so each degree of it is dimmer. Now a bounce through the ball finds the sun far more often, and the bright spot appears under the ball, still grainy. A bigger sun is a softer caustic and an easier one to find; a real one, small and bright, is the hard case.

From the light instead. Turn it round: start at the sun. A photon, a ray sent from the light, goes through the glass by Snell's law and Fresnel's (how much glass reflects and how much it lets through: chapter 10) with nothing to guess, and lands where the optics put it. Then the only question is whether the eye sees that point of the floor, and that is one straight line: a connection from the point to the eye, which adds the photon's light to the pixel the point lands in. This is light tracing. The strip shows it alone: the caustic, clean after a few samples, and a faint glow over the open floor, the light the ball's surface reflects there like a mirror, a caustic of its own. Nothing else, because a photon is connected only after it has passed glass or a mirror.

Both together: the camera's paths for everything they find well, the photons for the caustic. ShadyTracer divides the paths between the two (section 2): a path that reaches the sun through glass from the floor the eye sees first belongs to the photons, and the camera's side leaves it out. Everything else stays the camera's. Each path is counted by exactly one side, and the picture is all there. The bracket averages the bright spot with the ball's shadow on either side of it, which is why it reads less than the open floor even with the caustic in it: the spot itself is pixel 25, 0.83 after 400 samples.

Without that division, both sides count the caustic: the camera's chance finds and the photons. After 400 samples the bracket reads 0.738, against 0.313 with the split: the photons' 0.282 on top of the camera's own 0.455, about 2.4 times. (The camera's 0.455 is itself off the true value by the luck of its specks at 400 samples.) The open floor far from the ball reads the same either way, 0.395 (compare the readout's numbers with Both, split); the camera alone read it a little lower, 0.388, because the light the ball reflects there it finds only by chance. A renderer that adds a second way of finding light must take the light it finds away from the first.

The mathsWhat one connection adds to its pixel

A camera pixel is an average over the rays through it, spread evenly over its width on the film (chapter 1). A photon lands at a point x of the floor carrying light Φ (its share of the sun's). The camera would have seen x by a ray through some pixel, and what that pixel reads of x is the floor's radiance there, f × the light arriving, where f = ρ/2 in the flat world (ρ the floor's albedo, the share of light it sends back; the 2 spreads it over the half circle of directions it can go, chapter 8). So a connection adds

Φ × f × cos θxd cos²θc Δu

cos θx / d
the angle a piece of floor fills, seen from the eye d away, tilted θx from facing it: the flat world's 1/d (chapter 0)
1 / cos²θc
the film is flat, at distance 1 along the camera's axis, so towards its edge the same angle spreads over more film, twice over: a direction θc off the axis meets the film 1/cos θc away, which widens the angle's footprint by that, and meets it slanted, which widens it by that again
Δu
a pixel's width on the film; dividing by it turns "light landing on the film" into the pixel's average, as the camera's rays measure it

In 3D the angle becomes a solid angle, 1/d becomes 1/d², and the film's stretch 1/cos³θc: ShadyTracer's G = |n·v| d / z³, z the depth along the axis (section 4). The test holds this page's version to the camera's own pictures: a sunlit floor by photons alone reads what the camera's shadow rays read, pixel for pixel, and the caustic by photons what plain path tracing finds by chance, given long enough.

2Who counts what

The division is by the shape of a path, from the eye to the sun. A floor in the sun: eye, floor, sun. The camera's shadow ray finds it every time; it is the camera's.

The caustic: eye, floor, then the glass (once or more), then the sun. The first point the eye sees scatters light (a matte or rough surface: chapter 9's evaluable lobes), and every event after it is a delta. That exact class is the photons', and nothing else is. The camera's side tags a path as it forms: a camera path can still reach the sun this way, by the floor's bounce through the ball (section 1's lucky finds), and when a path of that class does, it adds nothing.

Now stand a pane of glass on the floor, between the eye and the ball. The floor seen through it: eye, glass, floor, sun. The camera gets there through the pane and aims at the sun from the floor. A photon could land on that floor too, but its connection to the eye would meet the pane, so it cannot count it: this stays the camera's.

And a caustic seen through glass: eye, glass, floor, glass, sun. The camera's shadow ray from the floor meets the ball; the photon's connection from the floor meets the pane, so light tracing cannot count it, and it stays the camera's. The camera finds it only by chance, as in section 1 before the photons: the floor's bounce through the ball, rare and bright, a shadow with specks that settles slowly (). ShadyTracer has a third way for exactly this, which meets the two in the middle: the photon map of chapter 19.

3Sending photons where they help

Where should photons start? The sun lights the whole scene, so ShadyTracer starts them evenly over a square across its rays, wide enough to cover the scene (here a line, its 24 cells drawn as bars of their chance). Most of them land on open floor, and a photon that has met no glass is not connected: the camera already counts that light. Of 1500 photons, 176 connect, about one in nine; the rest were work for nothing. (In 3D, where the ball is a far smaller part of a square than of a line, it was 1 in 115: section 5.)

So the renderer learns. Each cell keeps two counts, the photons it sent and the connections they made, and between samples its worth is connections per photon sent. Not connections alone: a cell already favoured sends more photons and would collect more connections for that reason only, and the map would feed on itself. The next sample's photons start in proportion to the cells' worth, and each is divided by that chance, chapter 8's rule, so the picture stays right while the photons move. (The step after one sample shows the map rebuilt from that sample's counts; the photons it drew were still sent evenly.)

After ten samples the photons crowd over the ball, and 919 of 1500 connect, 61%. A fifth of them still go evenly (a minimum share of 20%, dashed), so a caustic the map has not found yet is still found, slowly, and the picture is never wrong while it learns: every cell keeps a chance above 0, so every photon a cell sends is divided by a chance it really had, and no light is left with no way to be found. And both counts are halved at every rebuild, so the map forgets: it follows a moving sun or an edited scene within a few samples.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
The split is exact. The lt test on the developer's Ampere card, a scene with
        glass in the sun, read the picture's mean luminance within 0.03% of its reference on all eight rows: light
        tracing off and on, a tiny photon square, the map learning and learned, and NEE off with light tracing off and
        on (roadmap/PLAN.md, light tracing).

    

    

      
The map learns. Connections per photon went from 0.0087 with the photons spread evenly to
        0.605 once the map had learned, 70 times more useful photons, and the busiest 1% of cells took 72% of them (the
        same test, Ampere). In the caustic's own pixels the noise read 0.963 with light tracing off, 0.380 with an even
        map and 0.160 with the learned one at a tenth of a photon a pixel. Noise falls as one over the square root of
        the samples (chapter 12), so equal noise takes (0.963 ÷ 0.160)² ≈ 36 times fewer samples with the learned map
        (the entry rounds it to about 40), and (0.963 ÷ 0.380)² ≈ 6 with the even one (a later run,
        PLAN.md's open items; that entry does not name the card).

    

    

      
It costs. On the developer's GTX 1080 Ti (Pascal), 1280 × 720, the Noise library scene took
        736.5 ms a sample with light tracing and 508.8 without: 31% of the time
        (docs/PERFORMANCE.md, the benchmark's first full run). It is on by default, paired with NEE: the
        photons do the glass, the shadow rays the direct sun.

    

    

      
No direct light from the photons. A photon that meets no glass is not connected, though it
        could be: the camera's shadow rays find direct sunlight with less noise, so the split gives it to them (the
        first excerpt).

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A caustic nobody counted. With light tracing off and NEE on, the camera's weights for the
        sun seen through glass were 0, so a glass ball cast a plain shadow; and the pass that traces connections ran
        only with NEE on, so with NEE off the photons' light was lost. Both found when the developer looked for the
        caustic in all four combinations (PLAN.md; chapter 15 tells the first).
    • 
      
  • An emitter's caustic counted twice.
        :
        the camera's side tagged its paths only when the sun's photons ran, so with only glowing objects' photons
        running, both sides counted their caustics, 0.6% too bright.
    • 
      
  • Light tracing that switched itself off. Its photons share the rays' buffer with the camera's
        rays, and at about 2.3 million pixels there was no room left for them, so a large picture had no caustics; a
        sample is now traced in batches that make room (photons.js's header; chapter 23).
