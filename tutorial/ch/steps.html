Part VII · Speed · Chapter 26

Fewer and cheaper steps

A ray's time on the card is its steps, and a step's time is the scene's answer at one point: the distance to every object, of which the nearest is kept. This chapter is three ways ShadyTracer pays less for that. It skips the objects far from a ray, behind boxes. It makes a costly surface cheap away from itself, inside a shell. And it steps without asking the scene at all, from a grid of remembered distances. Each comes with what it saved on the user's cards, what it cost, and what was taken out again.

1Every object at every step

Sphere tracing, from chapter 2: a ray steps by the distance to the nearest surface (times 0.9, the step scale), and stops when that distance is below its tolerance. To know the nearest surface, the scene's function, which ShadyTracer writes from the scene tree (chapter 24), asks every object for its distance and keeps the smallest. So every step pays for every object, near the ray or not. Here is a shelf in the flat world: a floor, two plain discs, a rounded block, and two discs with a relief (chapter 3), a rough fine one and a broad smooth one, like the Noise library's marble and granite. 48 camera rays take 1,021 steps; each step asks all six objects, and each relief asks its noise, four layers of it. In this page's costs (one plain shape's distance is 1, a layer of noise 4: a model's numbers, the caption says why), the noise is 84% of the work. On the card, the Noise library's six reliefs were 89% of its time.

A culling box around each object: before asking the object, ask how far the box is, which is cheap and exact. Nothing inside the box can be nearer than the box itself, so while the box is farther than a margin (ten surface tolerances), the object is skipped and the box's distance stands in for it: the scene answers with the nearest of the objects it asked, or the nearest skipped box if that is nearer still. The cost falls from 38,798 to 12,545, about a third. Every ray ends where it did without the boxes; on the card, every picture with them was the same as without. On the user's Ampere card the Noise library ran 3.9 times faster with its boxes (216.6 against 839.8 ms a sample).

Two things a box costs. Its test: 4,730 here, one for every object at every step. And sometimes a step: where a box stands in, the ray may step only as far as the box, not as far as the object would have let it (387 of the 946 steps here). On the plain discs and the block, the box's test costs as much as the shape it guards, and its step is shorter; on a relief it is far cheaper than the noise, and its step longer (a relief's own distance is divided by its slope bound everywhere). So since 0.34.0, only the dear objects get a box: the reliefs, and every group. 907 steps, cost 12,208. On the user's Pascal card (the GTX 1080 Ti of chapter 25, as far as the documents show; these runs say only "Pascal"), boxes that never culled cost a third more time on scenes of plain shapes; boxes on the dear ones only were 13 to 15% faster than every box on the showcase, level on the demo, and 6% slower on the Noise library, where they still saved 72% against none. The user chose dear only.

One more turn. The reliefs today have a shell (section 3): far from its surface a relief costs no more than a plain shape. Switch it on: without boxes the shelf costs 10,702, and with boxes on the dear ones 11,471, 7% more. In this model, once the reliefs are shelled, their boxes no longer pay. Whether that is so on a card is not known: every culling measurement in the documents was made on the benchmark's frozen Noise library, whose reliefs have no shell. It is an inference, and the row that would test it (the shelled reliefs with their boxes and without) has not been run.

2Only at the root

The boxes guard only the scene's top-level objects and groups, where they are joined by the plainest join there is: the nearest wins, a union. There, a box's distance in place of what it holds is never more than the true distance to anything in the box, so a step is never too long; and it is always above the margin, so the march never takes a box for a surface. A group is boxed as a whole. Here two discs, A and B, joined into a lens (an intersection: where both are), boxed whole at the root: the rays find the lens. Drag P: the dashed circle is what the lens's own formula answers there, and wherever the box is skipped, the scene answers with the box's distance instead. That can be a little more than the formula's answer, which for a lens is itself an underestimate, but never more than the true distance.

Now box B on its own, inside the join, as ShadyTracer never does. Where B's box is far, B is skipped, and the lens is left as A alone: A's whole round surface, where the lens has none. Every ray stops on it, 1.2 short of the lens. A bound on B is not a bound on what B is joined into; the code's comment says it in one line: "in a subtraction, an intersection or a blend, a lower bound on a child is not a lower bound on the result".

A blend fails another way. Two discs a little apart, blended (chapter 3's smooth union): the blend builds a bridge between them, and the bridge lies outside B's own box. Skip B there, and the bridge is gone: two rays pass straight through where it stands, and a third slips past its edge to stop on A. At P, just above the bridge, the scene now answers 0.150 where the bridge is 0.054 away: a ray from P aimed at the bridge's nearest point would step past its surface. This is also why a group's box is padded: a smooth union can reach beyond what it joins, by at most a quarter of its blend each time it joins, and the padding adds that much.

3A surface that costs less from afar

Chapter 3 built a relief: a disc whose distance has noise added, four layers of it, and then divided by a slope bound so the march does not step through a bump. As the benchmark's frozen scene has it, every step pays for the noise and every step is divided, however far the ray is from the bumps. Fourteen rays at one relief: 318 steps, every one of them paying.

The division is the dearer half. The bound here is 1 + (0.17) × 17.2 ÷ 1.875, about 2.6 (amount over size, times the noise's steepest over its layers' total strength), so a ray far away moves 2.6 times slower than it could, and takes that many more steps to arrive, each with its noise.

The shell: the noise moves the surface by at most 1.386 times its amount (a bound proved in noise.js), so beyond twice that from the plain circle, the circle's own distance less that reach is a safe distance, with no noise and no division. 243 steps, 211 of them paying, and a cost a third lower. On the user's Ampere card the shell made the Noise library 22.9% faster (156.0 against 202.3 ms a sample); timed by kernel in the next run, the march 38% faster and the shadow rays 33%. The note written before that run expected most of the reliefs' cost to go. About a third of it went. The notes after it offered a reason, as a hypothesis: the boxes had already skipped the steps far from a relief, so the shell saved only the steps between a box's edge and the band, and the steps inside the band pay as before. The next run found the rest of the reliefs' time where that predicts, in the kernels that march, not in shading.

And a smaller bound. 17.2 is the steepest one layer of noise can be, measured, times four layers all at their steepest in the same place. A search of this four-layer noise itself found 8.9 at most in 14.4 million points; with a quarter to spare, 11.2. Divided by that, the steps are longer: 202 here. The benchmark has a row for it, and the relief keeps 17.2 until that row has been seen and timed by the user: a measured bound is not a proved one, and a bound too small makes holes.

4Steps without asking

A step needs the distance at the ray's point. The step cache remembers distances instead: a grid of cells over the scene's box, each holding the scene's distance at its centre. That distance is a ball with no surface in it, so a ray anywhere inside the ball can move to where it leaves the ball, without asking the scene. The cells are filled by a pass of their own before the rays are traced, and filled again only when the scene changes (a camera move keeps them: a ball does not care where the camera is). Here the shelf, boxed and shelled as today, with about 1,024 cells (57 × 19 = 1,083: the grid is rounded up to whole cells, as the engine's is): the darker a cell, the nearer a surface to its centre. A few of the balls are drawn.

Each step, the ray first looks at its own cell's ball. If the ball would carry it at least a cell further, it goes, to where it leaves the ball, times the step scale; otherwise it asks the scene, as ever. Not to the ball's nearest edge, as sphere tracing would: anywhere inside a ball, its exit along the ray is a safe step. And never on the last step of a march pass, which must end on the scene's own answer. 794 steps, 116 of them from the cache, and a tenth off the cost; every ray ends where it did without it. Point at a step to see its ball.

The second mode looks at the two cells ahead along the ray as well, and takes the farthest exit of the three balls: 755 steps here. On the user's Pascal card the cache, filled once, made the optics bench 27% faster, and three other scenes 6 to 10%; the demo read 4% slower, within that run's noise. On the dichroics scene, on the Ampere card, it changed nothing measurable. It is a developer setting, off by default.

The first fill asked the copy of the scene the rays march, the one with the culling boxes. Outside a relief's box, that copy answers with the box's distance, so the balls there stopped at the box, not at the relief. The user saw it in the cells' view: "I see orange glow around objects but also around their boundingboxes". Every ball was still safe, and most of those around the boxes smaller: 879 steps here, against 794. The fill now asks the copy made for picking, which has no boxes.
