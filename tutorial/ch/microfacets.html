Part III · What surfaces do · Chapter 9

Rough and shiny

Between the matte surface and the mirror of chapter 8 lies almost everything real: brushed metal, a varnished table, a plastic toy, whose reflections are blurred. ShadyTracer models them all one way, as a surface of tiny mirrors tilted at random. This chapter is that model (GGX), how the renderer draws a direction from it, and the two places it once went wrong in ways no straight-down test could see.

1A surface of tiny mirrors

Look closely at a shiny surface that is not a perfect mirror, and it is made of microfacets: flat pieces, each a mirror, far too small to see, tilted this way and that. Light reflects off each facet as off a mirror, and the tilts spread the reflections into a lobe round the mirror direction. The roughness says how tilted the facets are. Here a white metal of roughness 0.15 under light 40° from the normal (drag the light round): a narrow lobe, almost a mirror. The thin lines are directions drawn the way the renderer draws a rough reflection, shown flattened onto the page.

At roughness 0.6 the lobe is wide. And some of the drawn directions now point into the surface (drawn as blocked): reflected off one facet, they run into a neighbouring one. A single reflection loses that light; a real surface would bounce it again among its facets. One reflection keeps 0.8012 of the light here.

Light at a grazing 78°: the lobe leans and stretches along the surface, more of what is drawn hits neighbouring facets, and the facets facing the light are seen side-on.

ShadyTracer's facets follow the GGX distribution, the one most renderers use: a few formulas give how many facets point each way (D), and how many of those are hidden from the light or the eye by their neighbours (Smith's G). Its tilt, α, is the roughness squared: the roughness setting is the "perceptual" one most renderers offer.

The mathsThe lobe in four pieces

Light from ωi reflects towards ωo only off facets whose normal is halfway between, h = (ωi + ωo) normalised. The reflection's BSDF:

f = D(h) G2(ωo, ωi) F(ωo·h)4 cos θo cos θi

D
how many facets point along h, per unit of solid angle: GGX's 1 / (π α² (tan²θh/α² + 1)² cos⁴θh)
G2
the share of those facets seen from both directions, not hidden by neighbours: 1 / (1 + Λ(ωo) + Λ(ωi)), Λ(ω) = (√(1 + α² tan²θ) − 1)/2
F
the facet's own Fresnel reflectance, the colour of a metal (chapter 8) or the exact formula for glass and plastic (chapter 10)
4 cos θo cos θi
turns a density of facet normals into one of reflected directions, and the facets' area into the surface's

The renderer draws the facet normal h from the facets the eye can see, in proportion to how much of each it sees (visible normals), and reflects about it. The chance of the reflected direction then works out to G1(ωo) D(h) / (4 cos θo), and the weight f cos θi over that chance is F G2 / G1: never more than F, and exactly the share of light kept when averaged (section 2).

2The light a reflection loses, and giving it back

The share one reflection keeps, E, depends on the roughness and the view. The top chart gives it for a white metal at roughness 0.3, at every angle from the normal: close to 1 straight on and at grazing, lowest between. A real metal keeps the rest too, after a second or third bounce among its facets, so a white metal in a white furnace (chapter 8) should vanish. The single-reflection model would show it darker.

So ShadyTracer gives the light back. Before the first picture, the graphics card works out E for every roughness and angle into a table, and a metal's reflection is multiplied by 1 + F0(1/E − 1) (Turquin's compensation; for a white metal, 1/E). A white metal then reads exactly 1, if the table is right; with a table that is off, it reads Etrue / Etable, the bottom chart.

The table was off. It was built from a plain 32 × 32 grid of draws (the red dots), which at roughness 0.3 says 0.97850 seen 60° from the normal, against about 0.9751 converged (the comment in section 4 gives 0.97505): the reflections lost go below the surface from a thin rim of the draws the plain grid barely touched, so it saw too few of them. A white metal of roughness 0.3 read 0.997 in the furnace (the same comment). The grid is now squeezed towards that rim (the ringed dots): 0.97556, and the metal within 0.1% of 1.

At roughness 1 one reflection keeps less than half the light at 60°, and the compensation more than doubles it.

3A density that was right only straight on

Chapter 12's rule: a direction drawn with chance p is weighed by 1/p. If the code that draws and the code that states p disagree, every estimate is biased. ShadyTracer's old sampler used a formula for its density that is right when the light comes straight down. The chart is the old density over the true one for every reflected direction. Straight down it is 1 everywhere: correct.

At 60° it runs from 0.154 to 1.623. Where it was too large the weights were too small, and where too small too large: up to 6.5 times, a firefly. A clamp on the weights at 10 hid the fireflies and kept the bias. A test that looks straight down at a floor can never see this, which is why the renderer's material test looks at 70° off vertical.

At 80°, as low as 0.023: a weight 43 times too large.

One GGX, on the graphics card only. The tables are built on the graphics card by the very
        functions shading calls, so the share the compensation divides by is the share the lobe really has. "A JS copy
        of GGX would be a second model the first time either was edited" (microfacet.js's header): this
        chapter's figures use a copy of their own, which the renderer never sees.

        Etrue / Etable, so a row can be predicted without a graphics card.

        of finding a 5° sun by chance (the user's Pascal card, docs/TESTING.md, "BSDF parity").

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A density right only straight on, and a clamp at 10 that hid the fireflies it made: section
        3 (microfacet.js's header).
    • 
      
  • Three answers to "is it a mirror?", and an alpha clamped where the BSDF was evaluated but not
        where its density was: chapter 8's one threshold replaced them.
    • 
      
  • Every glass reflected 4%. Fresnel came from a "specular" slider, never from the index:
        "water, glass and diamond all reflected 4%, and total internal reflection jumped from 4% to 100%" (chapter
        10).
