Part III · What surfaces do · Chapter 9

Rough and shiny

Between the matte surface and the mirror of chapter 8 lies almost everything real: brushed metal, a varnished table, a plastic toy, whose reflections are blurred. ShadyTracer models them all one way, as a surface of tiny mirrors tilted at random. This chapter is that model (GGX), how the renderer picks a direction from it, and two places it once went wrong: a table of the light one reflection loses, and a sampler that was right only for light coming straight down.

1A surface of tiny mirrors

Look closely at a shiny surface that is not a perfect mirror, and it is made of microfacets: flat pieces, each a mirror, far too small to see, tilted this way and that. Light reflects off each facet as off a mirror, and the tilts spread the reflections into a lobe round the mirror direction. The roughness says how tilted the facets are. Here a white metal of roughness 0.15 under light 40° from the normal (drag the light round): a narrow lobe, almost a mirror. The lobe's distance from the point in each direction is how much light goes that way. The thin lines are 60 directions picked at random the way the renderer picks a rough reflection (chapter 8: pick at random, divide by the chance). They are directions in 3D, most of them a little out of the page, shown by their shadow on it, all drawn the same length: only where they point matters.

At roughness 0.6 the lobe is wide. And some of the picked directions now point into the surface (dashed, red, inside the metal): reflected off one facet, they run into a neighbouring one. A single reflection loses that light; a real surface would bounce it again among its facets. One reflection keeps 0.8012 of the light here.

Light at a grazing 78° (nearly along the surface): the lobe leans and stretches along the surface. The share lost is a little smaller here, 17.76% against 19.88% at 40°: how much a single reflection loses depends on the angle in a way the next section charts.

ShadyTracer's facets follow the GGX distribution, the one most renderers use: a few formulas give how many facets point each way (D), and how many of those are hidden from the light or the eye by their neighbours (Smith's G: masking and shadowing). Its tilt, α, is the roughness squared: a roughness slider that way feels even to the eye ("perceptual"), and most renderers offer it.

The mathsThe lobe in four pieces

Light from ωi (in) reflects towards ωo (out) only off facets whose normal is halfway between, h = (ωi + ωo) normalised. Every angle θ is measured from the surface's normal; θh is the facet normal's. The reflection's BSDF:

f = D(h) G2(ωo, ωi) F(ωo·h)4 cos θo cos θi

D
how many facets point along h, per unit of solid angle (per bit of direction): GGX's 1 / (π α² (tan²θh/α² + 1)² cos⁴θh)
Λ
how much of the surface a direction's neighbours hide, growing as the direction gets lower: Λ(ω) = (√(1 + α² tan²θ) − 1)/2, 0 straight down. A direction (x, y, z) in the surface's frame, z along the normal, has tan²θ = (x² + y²)/z², which is how the code writes it.
G1
the share of facets seen from one direction: 1 / (1 + Λ(ω))
G2
the share seen from both directions at once, not hidden by neighbours: 1 / (1 + Λ(ωo) + Λ(ωi))
F
the facet's own Fresnel reflectance: how much of the light a facet reflects at the angle it is met. F0 is that straight on, which for a metal is its colour (chapter 8); glass and plastic use the exact formula (chapter 10)
4 cos θo cos θi
turns a density of facet normals into one of reflected directions, and the facets' area into the surface's

The renderer draws the facet normal h from the facets the eye can see, in proportion to how much of each it sees (visible normals), and reflects about it. The chance of the reflected direction then works out to G1(ωo) D(h) / (4 cos θo), and the weight f cos θi over that chance is F G2 / G1: never more than F (before section 2's compensation), and exactly the share of light kept when averaged (section 2).

2The light a reflection loses, and giving it back

The share one reflection keeps, E, depends on the roughness and the view. It is an average: over all the directions the renderer might pick, of what each one keeps (0 for one that goes into the surface). The top chart gives it for a white metal at roughness 0.3, at every angle from the normal: 0.991 straight on, falling slowly, lowest near 85° (0.886) and rising again at the very edge. A real metal keeps the rest too, after a second or third bounce among its facets, so a white metal in a white furnace (chapter 8) should vanish. The single-reflection model would show it darker.

So ShadyTracer gives the light back. Before the first picture, the graphics card works out E for every roughness and angle into a table, and a metal's reflection is multiplied by 1 + F0(1/E − 1) (Turquin's compensation; for a white metal, 1/E). A white metal then reads exactly 1, if the table is right; with a table that is off, it reads Etrue / Etable, the bottom chart.

The table was off. Each entry is that average taken over a plain 32 × 32 grid of the two random numbers a pick is made from, in place of random ones (the red dots). At roughness 0.3, seen 60° from the normal, it says 0.97850, against 0.97505 worked out finely (the comment in section 4). Why: the second number, near 1, picks the facets the eye sees most edge-on, the most tilted ones, at the rim of what it sees, and theirs are the reflections that go below the surface. That rim is a thin strip of the grid, and a plain grid puts few points in it, so it saw too few of the losses. A white metal of roughness 0.3 read 0.997 in the furnace (the same comment).

The grid is now squeezed towards that rim (the blue rings): the second number becomes 1 − (1 − v)². The top tenth of the rim, from 0.99 to 1, now gets the grid's points from 0.9 to 1, ten times as many. Each of them then counts for less, 2(1 − v) times (0.1 at v = 0.95, where the strip is squeezed ten-fold), so the average is unchanged and only its error falls: 0.97556, and the metal within 0.1% of 1.

At roughness 1 one reflection keeps less than half the light at 60° (0.451), and only 0.307 straight on; the compensation more than doubles it. Here the loss comes from everywhere, not from a thin rim, so squeezing the grid buys nothing: both grids are off by up to about 0.15%, either way.

3A density that was right only straight on

Chapter 8's rule: a direction picked with chance p counts 1/p times (its probability density). If the code that picks and the code that states p disagree, every direction counts the wrong number of times, and the picture is biased: wrong in one direction, however many paths are averaged. ShadyTracer's old sampler used a formula for its density that is right when the light comes straight down. The chart is the old density over the true one for every reflected direction. Straight down it is 1 everywhere: correct.

At 60° it runs from 0.154 to 1.623. Where it was too large a direction counted too little, and where too small too much: up to 6.5 times, a rare pixel far too bright, a firefly. A clamp, a cap on any one count at 10, hid the fireflies and kept the bias. A test that looks straight down at a floor can never see this, which is why the renderer's material test looks at 70° off vertical.

At 80°, as low as 0.023: a weight 43 times too large.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
One GGX, on the graphics card only. The tables are built on the graphics card by the very
        functions shading calls, so the share the compensation divides by is the share the lobe really has. "A JS copy
        of GGX would be a second model the first time either was edited" (microfacet.js's header): this
        chapter's figures use a copy of their own, which the renderer never sees.

    

    

      
Predicted, then measured. A white metal on a sphere in a furnace shows exactly
        Etrue / Etable, so the furnace test can be predicted without a
        graphics card. Predicted 0.9998, 1.0006, 0.9971, 0.9999 for four metals; measured 0.9996, 1.0009, 0.9966, 1.0001
        (on ShadyTracer's developer's own graphics card, an Ampere, at 946 × 1061 pixels, 2026-09-29: chapter 0 says
        where such numbers come from). After the warp, the metals at roughness 0.5, 0.3 and 0.15 read 1.0003, 1.0002
        and 1.0002, each where its grid predicted (roadmap/SURFACES.md).

    

    

      
Picking and evaluating agree. A surface lit by the sun can be rendered two ways: by its
        picked directions alone, which find the sun by chance, or by also aiming a shadow ray at the sun and using the
        BSDF's formula (chapter 14). If the picking and the formula disagree, so do the two pictures. Seen 70° off
        vertical they agree to under 1%, in cd/m²: a plastic at roughness 0.3 18,557 against 18,635, a metal at 0.3
        3,439.7 against 3,447.0. A matte floor, whose cosine pick cannot be wrong, differs by as much, 0.79%: that is the
        noise of finding a 5° sun by chance (on the developer's older Pascal card, docs/TESTING.md, "BSDF
        parity").
