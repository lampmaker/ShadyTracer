A ray tracer in one page

Every flat figure in this tutorial runs a small path tracer. This chapter is that path tracer, whole: one file, tutorial/kit/onepage.js, 87 lines of code and no imports, read part by part. It finds surfaces by their distances, makes a picture from a camera, bounces light off matte walls and aims at a lamp. Then it is extended by a mirror, in four lines, and set beside ShadyTracer, part by part, to show where each piece grew up. Writing it also found a mistake in the tutorial's own figures, told in section 5.

1Finding the first surface

The world is the flat world of chapter 0: a slice, light spreading round circles. The room is a floor, a ceiling, two walls and a box, each a shape the program knows only by its distance: for any point, how far the nearest part of the shape is, negative inside (chapter 2). The lamp under the ceiling is a capsule: every point within 0.015 of a line. A ray leaves the eye and marches: at each step it asks every shape for its distance, and the smallest is a circle around the ray's point with nothing inside it, so the ray can safely jump that far. Here it reaches the box's side in 8 steps. Drag the handle on the ray to aim it.

Skim the box's top and the march slows down: along the top the box is always close, so every circle there is small (too small to see), and the ray takes 69 steps to get to where it hits. A real renderer pays for grazing rays the same way (chapter 2). The march stops when a distance is below 0.001 (a hit) or the ray is 50 away (a miss), and gives up after 200 steps. It steps 0.9 of each distance, not all of it: ShadyTracer's default step scale (), a margin for distances that are not quite exact and numbers that are slightly off (chapter 2).

Aimed up, the ray reaches the lamp in 9 steps. At a hit the program needs the surface's normal, the way it faces: the direction in which the hit shape's distance grows fastest, found by asking for the distance a hair to each side of the point across and up, four looks: central differences (chapter 4).

2From rays to a picture

    
The camera is a pinhole at the eye, its film 56 pixels across a field of view of 64°
      (chapter 1). Each pixel's ray goes through a random point across the pixel, so its samples cover all of it. One
      ray a pixel finds a surface; then the path bounces: a matte surface sends the light it
      receives evenly in every direction, so the program draws the next direction at random, more often near the normal
      where the cosine law says more light comes from (chapter 8), and multiplies the path's
      throughput by the surface's albedo, the share it keeps (0.7 here).
      A path that hits the lamp brings back its brightness, 1, times the throughput; most hit nothing glowing within
      four bounces and bring back 0. One sample a pixel: the picture's average 0.1118, and from pixel to pixel it jumps
      by 1.5 times the average, nearly all noise.

    
Each pixel is the average of its samples (chapter 12). 64 samples a pixel: the average
      0.1184, the jumps down to 0.22 of it. The noise falls as one over the square root of the samples: 64 is 8 times
      quieter than 1, and here 7 times.

    
Now aim at the lamp (chapter 14): at every bounce but the last, a
      shadow ray to a random point of the lamp, and if nothing is in the way, the light that
      point sends, divided by the chance of picking it. A path that then happens to hit the lamp adds nothing, or the
      lamp would count twice. 16 samples a pixel: the average 0.1190, the same picture as 64 without aiming within
      their noise, and its jumps 0.26 of it, nearly as quiet as 64 samples were, at a quarter of the paths.

    
The extension: the right wall becomes a mirror keeping 95% of the light. The eye sees the
      room again in it, the lamp included, and the picture's average rises to 0.1602. Section 3 is the code; it is the
      same rule as chapter 8's room.

Aiming at the lamp. The light a point of the lamp sends to a surface is the lamp's brightness, times the two
      cosines (how squarely the surface faces it, and how squarely it faces the surface), over the distance: the flat
      world's 1/r where 3D has 1/r² (chapter 0). The surface sends back albedo/2 of it (3D:
      albedo/π, chapter 8), and the pick is divided by its chance, 1 over the length it was drawn from.

3Extend it

    
The mirror is four lines inside the path, before the matte bounce: the ray reflected about the normal, the
      throughput times the mirror's albedo, and no shadow ray, because a mirror sends a lamp's light along one
      direction only, which a random point of the lamp would never lie on (chapter 8). So aimed is false,
      and a lamp seen in the mirror counts in full.

5What went wrong before

    
The first draft of this page aimed at the lamp's middle line, as the tutorial's figures then did, and its
      picture read darker with aiming than without. Two mistakes, both in the figures' own path tracer, both found by
      holding the two estimates against each other with enough paths:

    
    
      
  • The wrong lamp. The shadow rays went to points of the lamp's middle line, a lamp of no
        thickness, while the bounces hit the capsule, everything within 0.015 of it, whose outline is longer by the two
        round ends. In chapter 14's room, given twelve bounces, bouncing read 0.1326 ± 0.0014 and aiming 0.1213 ±
        0.0004, nine percent apart (computed, 44,800 paths each). Aiming now picks points of the outline the bounces
        can hit.
    • 
      
  • One bounce too many. The last surface of a path also aimed at the lamp, counting light one
        bounce further than a path without aiming can reach. Allowed one bounce, that room read 0.0870 aiming against
        0.0556 without. The last surface no longer aims.
    • 
    

    
The tutorial's test had a room checked both ways all along, and it passed: its lamp was 0.001 thick and its
      12,000 paths a pixel could not see a few percent. A check is only as good as the case it is given; chapter 29 is
      that lesson, and chapter 30's fattened lamp is the same mistake in the engine.
