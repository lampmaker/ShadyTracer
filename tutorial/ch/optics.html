Part VIII · Getting it right · Chapter 31

The optics bench

ShadyTracer also measures light the way a lighting lab does: not a picture, but numbers. Its Optics page traces a light's rays through a lens or a reflector and reports where the light went: how much leaves the fitting in each direction, how much falls on each part of a wall, how much each object absorbs. This chapter is how those numbers are made: rays binned by direction into intensity, hits on a surface turned into a map of illuminance, and every ray's light added up in integers, because a float sum cannot be trusted with millions of them. Which of its checks have passed on a real card, and which have not been run, is said for each.

1A light seen from far away

The optics trace starts rays at a light and follows each through every surface it meets: a photon (chapter 18) that never stops to make a picture. A ray that leaves the scene is counted by its direction alone, on the lighting trade's grid: γ, the angle from the light's axis, in 5° steps, and C, the angle around it, in 15° steps (24 to a turn). Light is counted in lumens (lm); the share of all directions a bin covers is its solid angle, in steradians (a whole sphere is 4π); a bin's lumens over its steradians is the intensity in that direction, in candela (chapter 5), as a lab's goniophotometer measures it from far enough away that the fitting is a point. Each of N rays carries 1000/N lm of a 1000 lm light, and a ring of bins between the angles γ1 and γ2 covers 2π (cos γ1 − cos γ2) steradians, a 24th of it each (farfield.js). The figure is a polar diagram: the light sits at its bottom point, pointing up, and the curve's distance from that point in each direction is the intensity that way (the rings are marked in candela). Here a matte (Lambertian) source of 1000 lm, whose true intensity is I0 cos γ, I0 = 318 cd (the fold-out says why; on a polar diagram cos γ is a circle through the light): 200,000 rays, and the curve follows the true one, spiky only near the axis. Its peak reads 362 cd, 14% high, and the beam angle, the width where the intensity falls to half its peak, 111° against the true 120°.

Why the peak reads high: like lines of longitude meeting at a pole, the bins crowd together near the axis (a small cap round it, then rings 5° wide, each cut into 24), so they are smallest there, catch the fewest rays and are the noisiest; and the peak is the largest of all the bins. The largest of many noisy numbers is high, by the noise. A narrower source, cos⁵ (its intensity falls as cos⁵ γ), at 20,000 rays: 34% high. The beam angle is read where the curve falls to half that peak; a peak too high puts that level too high, so the beam comes out too narrow: 44° against 59°. "The largest of many noisy bins reads high, and the beam angle inherits that", the optics module's own notes say.

Two million rays: the peak 2% high, the beam 57.8°. Noise falls only as one over the square root of the rays (chapter 12), so a hundred times the rays is ten times less of it.

For a round beam there is a cheaper remedy: every bin of a ring round the axis holds the same light, so they can be pooled into one, 24 times the rays each. The same 200,000 rays pooled: the peak 1% high, the beam 58.9° against 58.95°.

The maths318 cd, the beam angle, and 955 cos⁸ γ lux

The peak. A source whose intensity is I0 cosⁿ γ over the half of all directions it faces sends out I0 × 2π / (n + 1) lumens in all (adding the intensity over the half sphere's solid angle), so I0 = (n + 1) × 1000 / (2π): 318 cd for n = 1, 955 for n = 5.

The beam angle. Half the peak where cosⁿ γ = ½, so cos γ = 0.5(1/n) (the n-th root of a half), and the full width is twice that γ: for n = 1, cos γ = ½, γ = 60°, a beam of 120°; for n = 5, 2 × acos(0.5(1/5)) = 58.95°.

The floor. 1 m below the source, a point at angle γ off the axis is d = 1 / cos γ metres away, and the light reaches it slanted by γ, so its illuminance is I(γ) cos γ / d² (chapter 5) = 955 cos⁵ γ × cos γ × cos² γ = 955 cos⁸ γ lux.

2Light on a floor

A surface can be made a detector (lux, chapter 5: lumens falling on each square metre): every ray that reaches it stops there, and its landing point and light are recorded. The optics module turns the hits into a map: square cells over them, each cell's light over its area in square metres, which is illuminance in lux (chapter 5). Here the cos⁵ source of 1000 lm, 1 m above a floor, 50,000 rays on a floor 2 m square cut into 40 × 40 cells of 5 cm: about 29 hits a lit cell, so each cell is about 19% noise (one over the square root of its hits). The profile through the middle scatters round the true illuminance, 955 cos⁸ γ lux (the fold-out says why).

5,000 rays: 4 hits a cell, 51% noise, a map of noise. More rays is one way out.

The other is to smooth: let each hit count, a little, in its neighbours' cells too (a Gaussian bump around it, its spread, sigma, 1.5 cells, cut off at three times that). The noise falls to about 10% (the map's own estimate for a typical lit cell), but the map is now blurred over 8 cm, and at the spot's middle, where the light is sharpest, the blur reads low: over 120 runs its middle cell averages 913 lux, 4.4% under the true 955 (computed). This run's 831 is that bias and its own noise together. Less noise, more bias, the trade of chapter 29.

Or the photon map's own estimate (chapter 19): at each cell, the light of its 32 nearest hits over the disc they fill. Where hits are dense the disc is small, so an edge would stay sharp (this spot has none); where they are sparse it reaches further. Its noise is about 1/√32, 18% of each cell's own light, everywhere: small in the dim fringe, where the light is small, and still visible in the bright middle.

3Counting light exactly

An optics ray carries a weight: it starts at 1, its share of the light, and every surface that absorbs some lowers it (a white wall keeps 0.8 of it, say); nothing ends it at random. Every ray's weight has to be added into a total: what left the fitting, what each object absorbed. Thousands of GPU threads add at once, so the adds are atomic (chapter 23). A 32-bit float is a poor total for millions of rays. Its steps grow with the number (chapter 30): past 16,777,216 (2²⁴) the step is 2, and adding 1 rounds back to where it was. Here rays of weight 1: after 2²⁵ of them the float reads 16,777,216, half the light.

A weight of 0.7 goes wrong sooner: each add is rounded to the float's step at the total so far. While the total is between 1 and 4 million the step is ⅛ and then ¼, and 0.7 is added as 0.75, too much: the curve rises 4% above 1. Between 4 and 8 million the step is ½ and 0.7 is added as 0.5, too little, and the curve falls: after 8.4 million rays, a total near 5.9 million, the float is 5.8% low. Between 8 and 16 million the step is 1, 0.7 is added as 1, and the curve climbs back; past 16 million the step is 2, 0.7 rounds to nothing, and the total stops. And the result depends on the order of the adds: the same 100,000 random weights summed forwards and backwards give two different floats (the readout).

So the optics kernels count in integers: each weight is turned into 2²⁰ units per unit of weight, rounded once (0.7 is 734,003 units, 0.2 units short), and added into a pair of 32-bit whole numbers, like an odometer's two wheels: when the low one passes its largest value, 4,294,967,295, it wraps round to 0, and the code adds 1 to the high one by hand. Exact for any count it will ever see, in units: the only error is each weight's first rounding (the readout's 12.8 short after 67 million rays of 0.7 is 0.2 units each, a unit being 2⁻²⁰: 67 million × 0.2 / 2²⁰), the same in any order, and with no retries. A GPU adds whole numbers atomically but not floats (WGSL's atomics are integers only), so a float total needs a compare-and-swap loop (chapter 23): read the total, add, and write it back only if no other thread changed it meanwhile, else try again. When a narrow beam sends every ray to the same object, nearly every try fails and is retried, a "retry storm" in the code's words.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Weights, not survival. An optics ray carries what is left of its light; nothing ends it at
        random, and absorption only lowers its weight. Most path tracers play Russian roulette (chapter 13): a dim ray
        is ended at random and the survivors carry the light of the ones ended, which is right on average, but then any
        one run's totals move with the luck of the draw and the ended rays' light is in no tally. Here only a black
        surface, a glowing one, the bounce cap (its light counted as lost) or leaving the scene ends a ray. So the
        totals add up exactly, run by run: what was emitted equals
        what escaped, what the cap cut, and what each object absorbed, the last being what arrived at it less what left
        (optics/README.md).

    

    

      
Glass as the textbook says. The bench's refraction test, 400,000 rays a row: slabs of glass at
        0, 45 and 70° within 0.1% of what Fresnel's law says passes (chapter 10), total internal reflection exactly 1, a
        wedge's exit angle to 2 × 10⁻⁶ degrees; against an answer that treats light's two polarisations apart, the
        model's average of them off by 3% at 70°, as predicted (the user's Pascal
        card, 2026-09-27, docs/TESTING.md).

    

    

      
One readback for many batches. The optics module traces its rays in batches and reads each
        batch's totals back from the card to the page. A readback waits for everything sent to the card before it, the
        live picture's frames too: a Live round of four batches took 1.4 s with the picture running
        and 0.2 s with it stopped, at 1,000 rays as at 100,000 (the user, 2026-10-04). Now a group of batches is copied
        on the card and read back once (optics/README.md).

    

    

      
The far field, not yet run. The bench's test of this chapter's first figure, a Lambertian,
        a cos⁵ and a 30° cone against their beam angles, is built and has not been run on a card
        (docs/TESTING.md, optics-farfield: "open — first press"); the figure runs the same
        binning in JavaScript, not the kernel. Until version 0.67.3 the document gave cos⁵'s beam angle as 58.98°;
        2 acos(0.51/5) is 58.95° (computed), as the test itself expects, and the row says so now.

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A ray file read seven at a time. The old reader of Zemax ray files (a lens maker's format)
        ignored the field saying how many numbers a ray has, so a file with wavelengths was read seven numbers at a
        time, "garbage from the second ray on"
        ().
    • 
      
  • A colour from seventeen photons. A mix of three LEDs read a colour temperature (chapter 6) of
        4247 K where 4000 was right: the
        sensor was 3 × 3 pixels holding about 17 blue photons, where one error bar is 250 K. Read over the whole frame,
        about a million hits, it read 3999.9 K (docs/TESTING.md).
    • 
      
  • Batches flashing over the view. The drawn rays were whatever was in the trace's buffer last,
        and a Live analysis's batches would have drawn over them; each trace now publishes its paths to a copy of its
        own (optics/README.md).
    • 
      
  • Rays that switched themselves off. The live rays stopped at the first trace that failed, and
        "often" had to be switched on again (the user, 2026-10-28). A scene rebuilt under them can make a trace fail:
        the renderer puts the new scene in before its values are ready, and every structural edit, the background bake
        among them, opens that window. The Live analysis already waited it out and let such a round go; the rays now do
        the same, and stop only on a failure with the scene as it was (optics/page.js,
        showRays).
