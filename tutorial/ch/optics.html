Part VIII · Getting it right · Chapter 31

The optics bench

ShadyTracer also measures light the way a lighting lab does: not a picture, but numbers. Its Optics page traces a light's rays through a lens or a reflector and reports where the light went: how much leaves the fitting in each direction, how much falls on each part of a wall, how much each object absorbs. This chapter is how those numbers are made: rays binned by direction into intensity, hits on a surface turned into a map of illuminance, and every ray's light added up in integers, because a float sum cannot be trusted with millions of them. Which of its checks have passed on a real card, and which have not been run, is said for each.

1A light seen from far away

The optics trace starts rays at a light and follows each through every surface it meets: a photon (chapter 18) that never stops to make a picture. A ray that leaves the scene is counted by its direction alone, on the lighting trade's grid: γ, the angle from the light's axis, and C, the angle around it, in 5° steps. A bin's light over the solid angle it covers (chapter 5) is the luminous intensity in that direction, in candela, as a goniophotometer measures it from far enough away that the fitting is a point. Here a matte (Lambertian) source of 1000 lm, whose true intensity is I0 cos γ with I0 = 1000 / π = 318 cd: 200,000 rays, and the curve follows the true one. Its peak reads 362 cd, 14% high, and the beam angle, the width where the intensity falls to half its peak, 111° against the true 120°.

Why the peak reads high: near the axis the bins are smallest (a ring 5° wide round the axis cut into 24), so they catch the fewest rays and are the noisiest, and the peak is the largest of all the bins. The largest of many noisy numbers is high, by the noise. A narrower source, cos⁵, at 20,000 rays: 34% high, and since the beam angle is read at half that peak, it comes out 44° against 59°. "The largest of many noisy bins reads high, and the beam angle inherits that", the optics module's own notes say.

Two million rays: the peak 2% high, the beam 57.8°. Noise falls only as one over the square root of the rays (chapter 12), so a hundred times the rays is ten times less of it.

For a round beam there is a cheaper remedy: every bin of a ring round the axis holds the same light, so they can be pooled into one, 24 times the rays each. The same 200,000 rays pooled: the peak 1% high, the beam 58.9° against 58.95°.

2Light on a floor

A surface can be made a detector: every ray that reaches it stops there, and its landing point and light are recorded. The optics module turns the hits into a map: square cells over them, each cell's light over its area in square metres, which is illuminance in lux (chapter 5). Here the cos⁵ source of 1000 lm, 1 m above a floor, 50,000 rays on a floor 2 m square cut into 40 × 40 cells of 5 cm: about 29 hits a lit cell, so each cell is about 19% noise (one over the square root of its hits). The profile through the middle scatters round the true illuminance, I(γ) cos γ / d², which here is 955 cos⁸ γ lux.

5,000 rays: 4 hits a cell, 51% noise, a map of noise. More rays is one way out.

The other is to smooth: let each hit count, a little, in its neighbours' cells too (a Gaussian bump around it, 1.5 cells wide). The noise falls to about 10%, but the map is now blurred over 8 cm, and at the spot's middle, where the light is sharpest, the blur reads low: 831 lux against the true 955. Less noise, more bias, the trade of chapter 29.

Or the photon map's own estimate (chapter 19): at each cell, the light of its 32 nearest hits over the disc they fill. Where hits are dense the disc is small and edges stay sharp; where they are sparse it reaches further. Its noise is about 1/√32, 18%, everywhere.

3Counting light exactly

Every ray's light has to be added into a total: what left the fitting, what each object absorbed. Thousands of GPU threads add at once, so the adds are atomic (chapter 23). A 32-bit float is a poor total for millions of rays. Its steps grow with the number (chapter 30): past 16,777,216 (2²⁴) the step is 2, and adding 1 rounds back to where it was. Here rays of weight 1: after 2²⁵ of them the float reads 16,777,216, half the light.

A weight of 0.7 goes wrong sooner: each add is rounded to a whole step, and in the millions the step is 0.5 or 1, so 0.7 is added as one of those. After 8.4 million rays the float is 5.8% low. And the result depends on the order of the adds: the same 100,000 random weights summed forwards and backwards give two different floats (the readout).

So the optics kernels count in integers: each weight is turned into 2²⁰ units per unit of weight, rounded (0.7 is 734,003 units, 0.2 units short), and added into a pair of 32-bit words, the carry into the high word done by hand when the low word wraps round. Exact for any count it will ever see, the same in any order, and with no retries: a float total needs a compare-and-swap loop (chapter 23), which, when a narrow beam sends every ray to the same object, retries over and over, a "retry storm" in the code's words.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Weights, not survival. An optics ray carries what is left of its light; nothing ends it at
        random (no Russian roulette, chapter 13), and absorption only lowers its weight. Only a black surface, a
        glowing one, the bounce cap or leaving the scene ends it. So the totals add up exactly: what was emitted equals
        what escaped, what the cap cut, and what each object absorbed, the last being what arrived at it less what left
        (optics/README.md).

    

    

      
Glass as the textbook says. The bench's refraction test, 400,000 rays a row: slabs of glass at
        0, 45 and 70° within 0.1% of the formula, total internal reflection exactly 1, a wedge's exit angle to 2 × 10⁻⁶
        degrees; against the polarised answer, the unpolarised model off by 3% at 70°, as predicted (the user's Pascal
        card, 2026-09-27, docs/TESTING.md).

    

    

      
One readback for many batches. Reading a batch's numbers back waits for everything sent to
        the card before it, the picture's frames too: a Live round of four batches took 1.4 s with the picture running
        and 0.2 s with it stopped, at 1,000 rays as at 100,000 (the user, 2026-10-04). Now a group of batches is copied
        on the card and read back once (optics/README.md).

    

    

      
The far field, not yet run. The bench's test of this chapter's first figure, a Lambertian,
        a cos⁵ and a 30° cone against their beam angles, is built and has not been run on a card
        (docs/TESTING.md, optics-farfield: "open — first press"); the figure runs the same
        binning in JavaScript, not the kernel. The document gives cos⁵'s beam angle as 58.98°; 2 acos(0.51/5) is
        58.95° (computed), as the test itself expects.
