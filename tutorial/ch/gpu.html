A GPU for beginners

Everything so far could run on any processor, one ray after another. ShadyTracer runs on the graphics card, which does not work one ray after another: it runs one small program on thousands of rays at once. That changes what is fast and what is slow, what can go wrong, and how a program is handed its work. This chapter is the minimum needed to read the next three: what the card does with a kernel, how its memory is laid out, and how work is given to it, each with the trouble it caused ShadyTracer.

1Thousands of copies of one program

A kernel is one function. The card runs a copy of it for every item of work, each copy a thread told which item is its own, and here every pixel's ray is one. The threads are not independent: the card runs them in groups that take every instruction together, in lock-step, 32 at a time on NVIDIA's cards and 32 or 64 on AMD's. Each thread is one lane of its group. When the rays of a group need different amounts of work, the group takes as long as its slowest ray, and the lanes that finished early wait, idle. Here 128 rays march through a flat scene (chapter 2: each step moves a ray on by the distance to the nearest surface, so a ray passing close to an edge crawls). Each bar is one ray's march steps, and each box the time its group of 32 takes, as tall as its slowest ray. A lane-step is one lane held for one step: the boxes hold 4,000 of them, and the rays' 1,791 steps fill 45%. Point at a group to see its rays above.

Neighbouring pixels see neighbouring things and need similar work, so a group of neighbours wastes less. Shuffle the pixels and each group gathers rays from all over the picture, a slow one in nearly every group: 39%. ShadyTracer numbers its camera rays along the picture's rows (thread k makes pixel k's ray, at one ray a pixel), so a group of its threads is a run of neighbouring pixels.

The narrower the group, the less one slow ray holds up: in groups of 8 the lanes are busy 70% of the time. The card decides the width, not the program: the figure lets you choose it only to show what it does. What a renderer can do is keep the rays in a group alike, and stop a long loop before its slowest ray holds everyone up, which is what chapter 22 is about.

2Memory, laid out

A kernel reads and writes buffers, blocks of the card's memory. Two kinds matter here. A storage buffer is a large array the threads read and write: the rays, the picture. A uniform buffer holds the values every thread reads alike, the camera and the settings, read by a path the card keeps for exactly that; one may be at most 64 KiB (65,536 bytes). Its values are a struct, a list of named fields, each a type: f32 a decimal number, u32 a whole one from 0 up, i32 a whole one that may be negative, vec2, vec3 and vec4 two, three and four of one of those (vec3<f32> three f32s, vec3<u32> three u32s), and a matrix a small table of f32s. WGSL lays them out by a rule: each field starts at an offset (its first byte, counted from the struct's start) that is a multiple of its alignment, and the gap left before it is padding, never used.

So a vec3 takes 12 bytes but starts on a 16-byte boundary, and a single number after it fits in its row's last 4 bytes. This struct, in this order, takes 64 bytes, 8 of them padding: 4 before col, which must start at 48, and 4 at the end.

The same seven fields in another order, 80 bytes, 24 of them padding: t first leaves 12 bytes empty before pos, which must start at 16; uv after col leaves 4, since it must start at a multiple of 8; and its 8 bytes leave the last row half empty. Nothing is wrong, only wasted, and the kernel and the code that fills the buffer must agree on every offset. ShadyTracer does not write its uniforms' offsets by hand: tinywebgpu, its small WebGPU library, works them out from the field types (the second excerpt), and the tutorial's own test holds this figure's arithmetic to it. (A ray's size it does write by hand, 112 bytes, and a test holds that to the WGSL compiler's own.)

ShadyTracer's own uniform block, UB, before a scene adds its fields: 108 fields in 752 bytes, 36 of them padding. Its rows are drawn in eight columns to fit: read down the first column, then the next. Point at a box for its field. The scene's own values go on top of these, and the scene's records, when the scene is sent as data, have a uniform buffer of their own: 4096 vec4s, exactly the 64 KiB every WebGPU device allows (chapter 24).

3Giving it work

The program on the main processor asks the card to run a kernel by a dispatch: how many workgroups to run, each of a size fixed in the kernel (64 threads in ShadyTracer's ray kernels, 64 along x and 1 along y), counted along up to three dimensions. The graphics card runs a workgroup of 64 as two of its lock-step groups of 32 on NVIDIA's cards: the workgroup is the program's choice, the lock-step group the card's. Each thread works out its item from its group's place and its own: the thread at place 5 of workgroup 3 handles item 3 × 64 + 5 = 197. The simplest way over a picture is one long count: pixel 0, 1, 2 … along the rows, 64 to a group. At 1920 × 1080 that is 32,400 groups.

Every dimension of a dispatch may count at most 65,535 groups. 2048 × 2048 pixels is 65,536 groups of 64: one over. That is not a slow frame; it is a validation error, and nothing is drawn. The kernel that adds each sample into the picture (chapter 23's resolve) was dispatched this way, so it worked up to 65,535 × 64 = 4,194,240 pixels and no further: not at 2048 × 2048, one workgroup over, nor at a 4K picture (3840 × 2160, 8.3 million pixels), nor at a render sized to a 4K monitor.

One row per y instead: along x, the groups across one row (60 for a 4K row); along y, the rows (2160). A thread's pixel is then x = its group's x × 64 + its place, y = its group's y. Both far under the limit, at any picture a card can hold; the bars are drawn too short to see beside it, so each carries its number.

Dispatches are recorded and sent to the card together, a submission. If one runs too long, about 2 seconds on Windows, the system decides the card has hung and resets it, and the page loses its device (its handle on the card, which every buffer and kernel belongs to) and everything on it. ShadyTracer once sent a whole sample as one submission, and the Noise library, an example scene of shapes with noisy surfaces, hung (2026-09-29). Its sample had been timed at over a second, under the limit; that week a change to the march may have made it slower, which is ShadyTracer's own guess, not a measurement. A heavy frame is now cut into submissions of about 150 ms (the fourth excerpt).

        the card's own instructions; ShadyTracer's kernels are large. The user has two machines: a newer one with an

        the same document has the same kernels compiling in about 4 s there when timed one by one, and leaves the two

Eight storage buffers a kernel. WebGPU promises every device at least 8 storage buffers to
        one kernel; many cards allow more, but a kernel that needs a ninth will not run on one that does not. The
        shading kernel uses exactly 8, so what it needs from the kernel before it (the surface kernel, chapter 22) is
        written beside each ray's hit, in the list of hits it reads (the shade queue), instead of in a buffer of its
        own ().

    

    

      
Rays counted as memory. How many rays a sample may hold in flight is set as the memory they
        take, 1 GiB (2³⁰ bytes, about 1.07 billion), not as a count: a ray is 112 bytes in each of two ping-pong buffers (one
        read while the other is written, chapter 22), plus a 272-byte item in the shade queue, 496 bytes, so about 2.16
        million rays, enough for a 1080p picture (1920 × 1080, 2.07 million pixels) at one ray a pixel. Even so the shade
        queue alone is about 590 MB, over the 256 MB a device need allow one buffer, so the program still asks the card
        for its largest buffers when it starts. A sample with more rays and photons than that is traced in batches,
        chapter 23
        ().

    

    

      
Compiled while the page goes on. Making a pipeline (a compiled kernel ready to dispatch) the
        plain way holds the browser's main thread, the one that also draws the page (not one of the GPU's threads),

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A barrier not every thread reached. Threads of a workgroup can wait for each other at a
        barrier: none goes on until all have arrived. So every thread of the group must reach it, or the rest would wait
        for one that left, and WGSL refuses a kernel where that can happen (its uniformity rule). A guard, if (gid.x == N) { return; }, put
        before one let some threads leave first. The checker ShadyTracer runs without a GPU passed it, and the browser's
        compiler refused it on the user's machine
        ().
    • 
      
  • "24 modules parse." ShadyTracer's checker, a script run before every change that reads each
        JavaScript file (a module) without running it, said so on the day a kernel shipped with an empty string for
        its code, which failed to compile for everyone, and nothing noticed: it ran only with one setting on, and its
        compile failed long after the call that caused it
        ().
        Now every kernel is built against every scene by a stand-in device that records instead of compiling.
    • 
      
  • A buffer bound and then dropped. tinywebgpu binds what a kernel's text names (hands the card a
        buffer for each name); the compiled pipeline keeps only the names its entry point, main, reaches
        through the functions it calls. One kernel's text named the uniform buffer in a helper it
        never called, so the binding and the pipeline disagreed, and every frame with light tracing on was rejected
        ().
    • 
      
  • A dispatch while compiling. A redraw asked for during the first build dispatched a kernel
        still compiling: "No resources bound", the frame's commands rejected
        ().
    • 
      
  • Four million rays at every size. Before the 1 GiB, ShadyTracer allocated 4 million rays in
        three ray buffers and the hit buffer, about 1.7 GB with the ray's and hit's sizes of the time (both have grown
        since), whether the picture was 4K or 256 pixels. It worked only
        because the program asks the card for its largest buffers when it starts; on a device that gives the
        specification's defaults (256 MB a buffer), every one of those allocations failed
        ().
    • 
      
  • Out of x. Section 3, as it happened.
