A GPU for beginners

Everything so far could run on any processor, one ray after another. ShadyTracer runs on the graphics card, which does not work one ray after another: it runs one small program on thousands of rays at once. That changes what is fast and what is slow, what can go wrong, and how a program is handed its work. This chapter is the minimum needed to read the next three: what the card does with a kernel, how its memory is laid out, and how work is given to it, each with the trouble it caused ShadyTracer.

1Thousands of copies of one program

A kernel is one function. The card runs a copy of it for every item of work, each copy a thread told which item is its own, and here every pixel's ray is one. The threads are not independent: they run in groups that take every instruction together, in lock-step (32 at a time on NVIDIA's cards, 32 or 64 on AMD's). When the rays of a group need different amounts of work, the group takes as long as its slowest ray, and the threads that finished early wait. Here 128 rays sphere-trace a flat scene (chapter 2). Each bar is one ray's march steps, and each box the time its group of 32 takes: the lanes are busy 45% of the time. The tall bars are the rays that pass close to an edge, where sphere tracing crawls (chapter 2's grazing ray). Point at a group to see its rays above.

Neighbouring pixels see neighbouring things and need similar work, so a group of neighbours wastes less. Shuffle the pixels and each group gathers rays from all over the picture, a slow one in nearly every group: 39%. ShadyTracer numbers its camera rays along the picture's rows (thread k makes pixel k's ray, at one ray a pixel), so a group of its threads is a run of neighbouring pixels.

The narrower the group, the less one slow ray holds up: in groups of 8 the lanes are busy 70% of the time. The card decides the width, not the program. What a renderer can do is keep the rays in a group alike, and stop a long loop before its slowest ray holds everyone up, which is what chapter 22 is about.

2Memory, laid out

A kernel reads and writes buffers, blocks of the card's memory. Two kinds matter here. A storage buffer is a large array the threads read and write: the rays, the picture. A uniform buffer holds the values every thread reads alike, the camera and the settings, read by a path the card keeps for exactly that; one may be at most 64 KiB. Its fields are laid out by WGSL's rules: each starts where its alignment allows, and the whole is rounded up to 16 bytes. A vec3, three numbers, takes 12 bytes but starts on a 16-byte boundary, so a single number after it fits in its last 4 bytes. This struct, in this order, takes 64 bytes, 8 of them padding.

The same seven fields in another order: each single number pushes the next vec3 to the next row, and the struct takes 80 bytes, 24 of them padding. Nothing is wrong, only wasted, and the kernel and the code that fills the buffer must agree on every offset. ShadyTracer does not write its uniforms' offsets by hand: tinywebgpu, its small WebGPU library, works them out from the field types (the second excerpt), and the test holds this figure's arithmetic to it. (A ray's size it does write by hand, 112 bytes, and a test holds that to the WGSL compiler's own.)

ShadyTracer's own uniform block, UB, before a scene adds its fields: 108 fields in 752 bytes, 36 of them padding. Point at a box for its field. The scene's own values go on top of these, and the scene's records, when the scene is sent as data, have a uniform buffer of their own: 4096 vec4s, exactly the 64 KiB every WebGPU device allows (chapter 24).

3Giving it work

The program on the main processor asks the card to run a kernel by a dispatch: how many workgroups to run, each of a size fixed in the kernel (64 threads in ShadyTracer's ray kernels), counted along up to three dimensions. Each thread works out its item from its group's place and its own. The simplest way over a picture is one long count: pixel 0, 1, 2 … along the rows, 64 to a group. At 1920 × 1080 that is 32,400 groups.

Every dimension of a dispatch may count at most 65,535 groups. 2048 × 2048 pixels is 65,536 groups of 64: one over. That is not a slow frame; it is a validation error, and nothing is drawn. The kernel that adds up each sample into the picture (chapter 23) was dispatched this way, and stopped working at 4,194,240 pixels: 2048 × 2048 by one workgroup, a 4K picture, a render sized to a 4K monitor.

One row per y instead: along x, the groups across one row (60 for a 4K row); along y, the rows (2160). Both far under the limit, at any picture a card can hold.

Dispatches are recorded and sent to the card together, a submission. If one runs too long, about 2 seconds on Windows, the system decides the card has hung and resets it; the page loses its device and everything on it. ShadyTracer once sent a whole sample as one submission, and its heaviest scene took over a second a sample: it hung. A heavy frame is now cut into submissions of about 150 ms (the fourth excerpt).
