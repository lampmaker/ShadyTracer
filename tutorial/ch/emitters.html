Part IV · Path tracing · Chapter 16

Every glowing thing is a light

A lamp placed with the scene builder is a light the renderer knows about: a rectangle or a disc, its size and place, aimed at directly (chapter 14). But any shape can glow: give a sphere, a twisted tube or a whole wall a glowing material, and it lights the scene too. To aim at those, the renderer first has to find them, without being told where they are or what shape they have.

1Found by the paths that hit them

Paths bounce through this room as in chapter 13. When one hits something that glows, it notes where: in which cell of a coarse grid over the scene (16 × 16 here; 16 × 16 × 16 in ShadyTracer), widening that cell's box, the tightest box round every hit it has had, and keeping the brightest glow seen there. A cell with hits becomes a target for aiming, a circle round its box. After one sample of 48 paths, 28 have hit the ball in plain view; 2 have hit the bar behind the wall, by bouncing over it.

After 8 samples both are listed, in a few cells each: from now on the renderer aims at them, as at a lamp, picking a cell (brighter and larger ones more often) and then a direction into its circle.

And before? A glowing thing nobody has found yet is lit only by the paths that happen to hit it, exactly as in chapter 13: the aiming strategy's chance of picking its directions is 0, so under MIS (chapter 15) those hits keep their full weight. Nothing is counted twice and nothing is lost: the picture is right from the first sample, only noisier until the glow is found. The code says it in one line: "That is why this is unbiased from the first sample".

2Random lines

Light tracing (chapters 18 and 19) needs the opposite: light leaving a glowing surface, from random points of it, in random directions, as many from each part as it gives off. For a shape known only by its distance formula, picking a random point on its surface is hard. ShadyTracer uses a fact of geometry instead. Draw random lines through a circle round the shapes (a random direction, a random offset from the middle). Where a line crosses a surface, start there.

Count the crossings and each shape is crossed in proportion to the length of its outline (the readout: 17,972 crossings of the circle against 18,000 predicted from its length alone). And the angles the lines cross at (the plot) are spread exactly as a matte glowing surface sends out its light: most straight out, fewer at a slant, in proportion to the cosine (the dots). So the crossings are points spread evenly over every surface, with directions spread the right way, for a shape the method knows nothing about. In 3D it is the same with lines through a sphere and the area of the surface: the Cauchy–Crofton formula.

The mathsLines through a circle

A line is picked by an angle θ in [0, π) and an offset p in [−R, R], both uniformly: all lines through the circle have measure 2πR. The lines crossing a short piece of curve ds at angle φ from its normal fill an offset width |cos φ| ds for each direction, so

expected crossings of a curve of length ℓ = 2ℓ2πR = ℓπR a line

and at each crossing the angle φ has density ½ cos φ, which is the flat world's matte emission. In 3D a line through a sphere of radius r crosses a piece of surface dA in direction ω with density |n·ω| dA dω / (4π²r²), which is the matte emission measure itself.

3How bright, in 32 bits

The grid keeps the brightest glow each cell has seen. Thousands of paths write to it at once, so it is kept with an operation the GPU can do from every thread safely, "keep the larger", which works on whole numbers. So the brightness has to become a whole number whose order is the brightness's order. It used to be the brightness times 16, capped at 100,000: fine while glowing materials were "5 per channel", useless once they were in real units, candela per square metre (chapter 5), where a bright panel is 20,000 and the sun seen in a mirror 1.6 billion, and everything above the cap ranked equal. Below 1/16 it was 0, the same as never seen. The log code keeps every brightness from 2⁻⁴⁰ to 2⁴⁰ in 24 bits, in order.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Aiming at found glow pays. On the showcase scene lit only by a glowing sphere, aiming at the
        discovered glow cost 2.3 times as much per sample and still reached a given noise 1.4 times sooner than not
        aiming; light tracing cost 47% more per sample and bought nothing there (roadmap/PLAN.md, "Emissive
        surfaces as lights", second run; the first run was on an Ampere card).

    

    

      
Too many glows is noisier, never wrong. At most 64 boxes are listed. A glow past that is not
        aimed at, and is lit by the paths that hit it, as before it was found (the comment above the collect kernel).

    

    

      
The list only changes between samples. If it moved during a sample, the density a shadow ray was
        drawn with would differ from the one a later bounce is weighed by, and MIS's weights would no longer sum to
        one.

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A code that saturated: the linear brightness code of section 3.
    • 
      
  • Glow counted twice. In the first run on hardware, light tracing read 0.6% brighter than
        without it: camera paths were marked for the split with the photons only while the sun's photons existed, so
        with the sun off, a glowing sphere's caustics were counted by both.
    • 
      
  • Two estimates of two things: aiming evaluated its own approximation of the materials while
        bounces sampled the real ones, so brightness moved with the share of shadow rays (chapter 15).
    • 
      
  • A sum that overflowed. The cell keeps the brightest glow, not the sum: a glow filling the
        screen is a million hits a sample, and their sum would wrap round a 32-bit number within one (the comment in
        emitDeposit).
