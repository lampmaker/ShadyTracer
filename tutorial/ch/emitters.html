Part IV · Path tracing · Chapter 16

Every glowing thing is a light

A lamp placed with the scene builder is a light the renderer knows about: a rectangle or a disc, its size and place, aimed at directly (chapter 14). But any shape can glow: give a sphere, a twisted tube or a whole wall a glowing material, and it lights the scene too. To aim at those, the renderer first has to find them, without being told where they are or what shape they have.

1Found by the paths that hit them

Paths bounce through this room as in chapter 13. When one hits something that glows, it notes where: in which cell of a coarse grid over the scene (the dashed grid, 16 × 16 here; 16 × 16 × 16 in ShadyTracer), widening that cell's box, the tightest box round every hit it has had (the small rectangles), and keeping the brightest glow seen there. A cell with hits (shaded) becomes a target for aiming: the dashed circle round its box. After one sample of 48 paths, 28 have hit the ball in plain view; 2 have hit the bar behind the wall, by bouncing over it. The faint lines are the latest paths that found a glow: blue while they bounce, orange on the piece that hits it.

After 8 samples both are listed, in a few cells each: from now on the renderer aims at them, as at a lamp, picking a cell (brighter and larger ones more often) and then a direction into its circle. Drag the ball behind the wall, or into a corner, and see how many samples it takes to be found.

After 64 samples, every part of both glows that paths reach has a cell.

And before a glow is found? It is lit only by the paths that happen to hit it, exactly as in chapter 13, and that is still right. Take a bounce whose direction meets the bar before the bar is listed. Under MIS (chapter 15) its weight is pplate² / (pplate² + plamp²), and the aiming strategy's density plamp for that direction is 0, since nothing aims at the bar yet: the weight is 1, and the hit counts in full. Once the bar is listed, the same direction gets a weight between 0 and 1, and the aiming strategy brings the rest. Either way the light is counted once: the picture is unbiased from the first sample, only noisier until the glow is found. The code says it in one line ().

2Random lines

Section 1 traced from the eye and waited to hit a glow. Light tracing (chapters 18 and 19) goes the other way: it starts light at a glowing surface and follows it out, from random points of it, in random directions, as many from each part as it gives off. That needs a random point on the surface, and for a shape known only by its distance formula (chapter 2: how far is the nearest surface, nothing more) there is no list of its surface to pick from. Finding where a line crosses a shape is easy, though: it is what the renderer does all day, marching along a ray. So ShadyTracer uses a fact of geometry. Draw random lines through a circle round the shapes (a random direction, a random offset from the middle); in ShadyTracer that circle is each found box's sphere from section 1. Where a line crosses a glowing surface, start a photon there, leaving the surface along the line.

Count the crossings and each shape is crossed in proportion to the length of its outline: with 40,000 lines through the dashed circle (radius 2), the disc (radius 0.45) is crossed 17,972 times, and its length alone predicts 18,000. And the angles the lines cross at (the bars) are spread exactly as a matte glowing surface sends out its light: most straight out along its normal (the direction straight out of the surface), fewer at a slant, in proportion to the cosine of the angle from the normal (the dots; this is Lambert's law for a matte surface). So the crossings are points spread evenly over every surface, with directions spread the right way, for a shape the method knows nothing about. In 3D it is the same with lines through a sphere and the area of the surface: the Cauchy–Crofton formula.

The mathsLines through a circle

A line is picked by an angle θ in [0, π) and an offset p in [−R, R], both uniformly. θ stops at π because a line has no front: turned by half a turn it is the same line. So the lines through the circle fill a range π × 2R = 2πR. Now a short piece of curve, of length ds. Lines at angle φ from its normal see it as a width |cos φ| ds across them (its shadow in their direction), so that width of offsets crosses it; added up over every angle, |cos φ| over a half turn adds up to 2. The lines crossing the piece fill a range 2 ds, out of 2πR, and a curve of length ℓ is crossed, on average,

expected crossings of a curve of length ℓ = 2ℓ2πR = ℓπR a line

times a line. The 2 is that sum of |cos φ|. And because each angle's lines cross in proportion to cos φ, the angles at the crossings come out spread as ½ cos φ, which is the flat world's matte emission. In 3D a line through a sphere of radius r crosses a piece of surface dA in direction ω with density |n·ω| dA dω / (4π²r²), which is the matte emission measure itself.

3How bright, in 32 bits

The grid keeps the brightest glow each cell has seen, in one 32-bit whole number a cell. Thousands of paths, each on its own thread of the GPU, write to it at once. Two that read the old value at the same moment and each write their own would lose one of the two, so the grid is kept with an operation the GPU does in one step for every thread safely, "keep the larger" (atomicMax), which works on whole numbers. So the brightness has to become a whole number whose order is the brightness's order.

It used to be the brightness, capped at 100,000, times 16: fine while glowing materials were "5 per channel" (80 codes), useless once they were in real units, candela per square metre (chapter 5), where a bright panel is 20,000 and the sun seen in a mirror 1.6 billion, and everything above the cap ranked equal. Below 1/16 it was 0, the same as never seen. On the figure's scale, where each step is ten thousand times the last, that old code hugs zero, climbs over the last steps before 100,000 and is flat after.

The log code counts doublings instead: every doubling of brightness gets the same number of codes, 209,715 (a doubling is what photographers call a stop). 80 stops, from 2⁻⁴⁰ to 2⁴⁰, fill 2²⁴ codes, in order. The comment does not say why 2²⁴ and not all 32 bits; a likely reason (inferred) is that the code is worked out in a 32-bit float, which counts whole numbers exactly only up to 2²⁴ (chapter 12).

4How ShadyTracer does it

The deposit, from every path that hits a glowing surface:


    

    

      
A number in the comment, checked. The comment gives the log code's step as "2e-5 of a stop".
        From its own constants it is 80 stops over 2²⁴ codes, 4.8 × 10⁻⁶ of a stop: finer than it says.

    

    
Between samples, each cell with hits becomes a box with a sphere round it (the figure's circles), scored by its
      brightest glow times its size squared (roughly its power), and the scores become the chances of picking each.
      Aiming (chapter 14's NEE) then picks a box and a direction uniformly within the cone its
      sphere fills as seen from the point. Both halves of MIS ask for the density of that choice: its chance
      per unit of direction, chapter 15's plamp. Directions in 3D are measured by solid
      angle, the area a bundle of them covers on a sphere of radius 1 (the whole sphere is 4π); a cone of half-angle
      α, the angle from its middle to its edge, covers 2π(1 − cos α).

    

    
And the lines for light tracing, Crofton's density, which needs nothing about the shape but the spheres. A photon
      stands for all the light leaving its patch of surface along its line; lines come through that patch at a rate given
      by this density, so each photon's brightness is divided by it, chapter 12's rule again: what is picked often counts
      for little each time.

    

  






  

    
5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Aiming at found glow pays. On the showcase scene lit only by a glowing sphere, aiming at the
        discovered glow cost 2.3 times as much per sample and still reached a given noise 1.4 times sooner than not
        aiming; light tracing cost 47% more per sample and bought nothing there (roadmap/PLAN.md, "Emissive
        surfaces as lights", second run). The document names no card for that run; the run before it was on the user's
        NVIDIA Ampere.

    

    

      
Too many glows is noisier, never wrong. At most 64 boxes are listed. A glow past that is not
        aimed at, and is lit by the paths that hit it, as before it was found (the comment above the collect kernel).

    

    

      
The list only changes between samples. If it moved during a sample, the density a shadow ray was
        drawn with would differ from the one a later bounce is weighed by, and MIS's weights would no longer sum to
        one.

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A code that saturated: the linear brightness code of section 3.
    • 
      
  • Glow counted twice. Light from a glowing surface that reaches the camera through glass (a
        caustic) can be found two ways: by camera paths, and by photons
        from the surface (chapters 18 and 19). The renderer splits the work: camera paths are marked to leave that light
        to the photons. In the first run on hardware the marking was made only while the sun's photons existed, so with
        the sun off both counted it, and the picture read 0.6% too bright.
    • 
      
  • Two estimates of two things: aiming evaluated its own approximation of the materials while
        bounces sampled the real ones, so brightness moved with the share of shadow rays (chapter 15's last story).
    • 
      
  • A sum that overflowed. The cell keeps the brightest glow, not the sum: a glow filling the
        screen is a million hits a sample, and their sum would wrap round a 32-bit number within one (the comment in
        emitDeposit).
