Part III · What surfaces do · Chapter 8

Diffuse and mirror

What a surface does with the light that reaches it is its material. The two simplest are opposites: a matte surface, paper or chalk, scatters light every way, and a mirror sends all of it one way. Every material in ShadyTracer is built from pieces like these two, and how each one is drawn at a bounce decides whether a picture is right, and how noisy it is.

1Matte: the same from every side

A material is described by its BSDF, written f: for light arriving from one direction, how bright the surface looks from another. A matte surface (a Lambertian one, after Lambert, whose cosine law of chapter 1 it follows) has the simplest f there is, a constant. It looks equally bright from every direction: you cannot tell where you stand from how bright a sheet of paper is. Its albedo ρ is the share of the light it sends back, 0.7 here; the rest it absorbs.

The lamp (drag it round) is 35° off the point's normal and 2.6 away. It puts E = 0.3151 on the point (its illuminance): cos 35° / 2.6, since in the flat world light spreads round a circle and so falls as one over the distance, not its square (chapter 0). The floor sends back 0.7 of that, and spreads it so that it looks the same from everywhere, 0.1103 (point anywhere above the floor to read it).

Two words keep apart what "bright" means here (chapter 5). How bright the floor looks is its luminance: light per bit of the surface as you see it, the same from every side. Its intensity is the light the whole patch sends one way: its luminance times the area you see, and from a slant the same patch looks narrower, by the cosine. So the intensity is largest straight up and falls towards the floor: the dashed circle, whose distance from the point in each direction is that intensity. A circle through the point is exactly the cosine: from a point on a circle, its centre straight above, the chord in a direction θ from straight up is the diameter times cos θ (the angle in a semicircle is a right angle).

Move the lamp to 75°. Less light arrives (0.0995, the cosine law again), so the floor is dimmer, but still equally bright from every side: the circle shrinks, and keeps its shape.

Picking at random, and dividing by the chance. A renderer follows light backwards: a path starts at the eye, and at each surface it meets it must ask how much light arrives from every direction above, far too many to try. So it picks one direction at random, follows it, and lets the many paths through a pixel average out (chapters 12 and 13). For that average to be right, a direction picked with chance p counts 1/p times. It is how you would guess a class's total pocket money by asking one pupil picked at random: multiply the answer by 30, which is dividing by the chance 1/30 of picking that pupil; if tall pupils were picked twice as often, you would divide their answers by their larger chance, and on average the guess would still be right. Here the chance is per bit of direction, a probability density.

The thin blue lines are how the renderer picks a matte bounce: one direction a bounce, crowded near the normal in proportion to the cosine. They do not depend on where the lamp is: a matte surface scatters the same whichever way the light came, so the renderer picks the same either way. A direction picked like that passes on f cos θ divided by its own chance, and for a matte surface that is exactly ρ (the fold-out): every matte bounce multiplies a path's throughput, the share of the light found further on that still reaches the eye, by its albedo and nothing else. (It is f cos θ because a surface's light out is f times the light arriving, and the light arriving from a direction counts by its cosine: chapter 1's slant.)

The mathsWhy ρ/π, and why the cosine draw weighs ρ

The light going out towards ωo (o for out) is the light arriving from every direction ωi (i for in), each times f and its cosine, added up over the half-sphere above the point. The ∫ … dω is that adding up: over every small patch of directions, each counted by its size (its solid angle):

Lo(ωo) = ∫ f(ωi, ωo) Li(ωi) cos θi dωi

A matte f is a constant, so it comes out of the adding up. Lit by a sky of luminance 1 every way, the point receives π (chapter 5's fold-out: the whole half-sphere pressed onto the floor is a disc of area π), and the formula says it looks fπ. On the other hand it sends back ρ of what it receives, ρπ in all, and a surface that looks L from everywhere sends out πL in all (the same disc, turned round), so it looks ρπ/π = ρ. Both are true: fπ = ρ, and f = ρ/π. In the flat world the half-circle pressed onto the floor is a line 2 long, so f = ρ/2.

The renderer picks ωi with chance per unit of direction p = cos θ/π: the cosine, divided by π because the chances over every direction must add up to 1, and the cosine adds up to π over the half-sphere (the pressed disc again). It is made by running the pressed disc backwards: pick a point evenly in the floor's unit disc (at radius √u for a random u, which keeps it even) and lift it straight up onto the dome. One picked direction stands for the whole sum as (f cos θ / p) Li, the pocket-money rule above (chapter 12 shows the average comes out right), and f cos θ / p = (ρ/π) cos θ / (cos θ/π) = ρ.

A mirror has no such p: all its light goes one way, all the chance on one direction, an infinite density there and 0 everywhere else. It is called a delta, and it can only be followed, never aimed at.

2A mirror, which nothing can aim through

A room lit by a lamp near its ceiling, rendered as chapter 14 renders it: at every matte bounce a shadow ray aims at a random point of the lamp. The right wall is matte. The lamp shines with a brightness of 1, every wall keeps 0.7 of the light at each bounce, and the strip below is the eye's picture, 56 pixels (pixel 1 looks highest, towards the ceiling; pixel 56 lowest, at the floor), its average 0.1316 once 96 paths a pixel are in (a sample is one path through every pixel, so 96 samples). A box stands on the floor.

Now the right wall is a mirror reflecting 95% of the light. The eye sees the room again in it, the lamp included: pixel 26 reads 0.950, the lamp's 1 times the mirror's 0.95. No shadow ray found that. At a mirror a path has no choice to make, it goes on along the reflection, and a shadow ray from the mirror to a random point of the lamp would almost never lie along it. So the renderer does not aim at a mirror bounce; the path finds the lamp, or not, by going on, and counts what it finds in full. At a matte bounce it is the other way round: the shadow ray has already counted the lamp, so a bounce that happens to hit it adds nothing, or the lamp would be counted twice (chapter 15 is the full rule for when both ways can).

In ShadyTracer a metal's colour is its reflectance straight on (how much of each colour it reflects when you look at it square on), and towards grazing, when you look along it, every metal reflects more, up to all of it (Schlick's formula, below).

And any surface smoother than a roughness of 0.0316 is treated as a perfect mirror: below that, chapter 9's blurred reflection is narrower than the renderer can tell apart from one direction (its march finds surfaces only to within a small tolerance).

3The white furnace

How do you know a material keeps the light it should? Put it in a white furnace: a sky of brightness 1 in every direction and nothing else. A surface of albedo 1 sends back all it receives, so wherever the eye looks it sees 1, and the shapes vanish (a real test uses a sky of 1000 cd/m²; only the share that comes back matters). Here two discs, close together so that light bounces between them: every pixel reads exactly 1. Not about 1: every path, through every pixel, brings back 1, since each matte bounce weighs ρ = 1 and every path ends in the sky. Switch to Mirror: the same.

At an albedo of 0.7 they appear: each bounce keeps 0.7, the gap between them, where light bounces more than once, darkest (bracketed on the strip: the eye sees only the gap's mouth, pixels 17 to 19, since the left disc hides the rest of it). The picture's average is 0.8128.

At albedo 1 again, but with paths stopped after one bounce: the light that needed a second bounce to escape the gap is lost, and the gap shows: its pixels read down to 0.83, the picture's average 0.9963. A furnace finds a renderer's losses as well as a material's.

And a black pair, albedo 0, as a control: it must show. A test that only ever passes might not be looking at all. ShadyTracer's furnace test gained a black sphere after a run in which it passed for every material, and no sphere had been drawn: the browser had mixed old copies of the renderer's code with new ones, and every pixel showed the sky (section 6).

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A matte floor that was a plastic. makeDiffuse used to carry a 4% shiny coat:
        "a plastic", which "cost 2.5% of its light". Chapter 5's white floor read 0.975 of ρE/π until it
        went.
    • 
      
  • A diffuse term that lost energy. The matte part under a coat was weighted by what the coat
        did not reflect, and the energy a rough coat loses was given back to nobody: a white matte floor "could not pass
        a white furnace" (microfacet.js's header). The base is now coupled to the coat's own reflectance,
        so whatever the coat does not send back, the base gets.
    • 
      
  • A furnace that read the sky. On 2026-09-29 a page mixing cached old code with new read
        1000.000244 with no noise on every row: no sphere had been drawn at all. The test now has a black sphere that
        must read well below 1 (the comment above it in lib/pathtracer/tests.js).
