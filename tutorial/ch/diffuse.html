Part III · What surfaces do · Chapter 8

Diffuse and mirror

What a surface does with the light that reaches it is its material. The two simplest are opposites: a matte surface, paper or chalk, scatters light every way, and a mirror sends all of it one way. Every material in ShadyTracer is built from pieces like these two, and how each one is drawn at a bounce decides whether a picture is right, and how noisy it is.

1Matte: the same from every side

A material is described by its BSDF, written f: for light arriving from one direction, how bright the surface looks from another. A matte surface (a Lambertian one, after Lambert, whose cosine law of chapter 1 it follows) has the simplest f there is, a constant. It looks equally bright from every direction: you cannot tell where you stand from how bright a sheet of paper is. Its albedo ρ is the share of the light it sends back, 0.7 here; the rest it absorbs.

The lamp (drag it round) is 35° off the point's normal. It puts 0.3151 on the point (the flat world's cos 35° / 2.6, chapter 0); the floor sends back 0.7 of that, and spreads it so that it looks the same from everywhere, 0.1103 (point anywhere above the floor to read it). Its intensity going out, though, is largest straight up and falls with the cosine towards the floor, because from a slant the same bright patch looks narrower: the dashed circle, whose distance from the point in each direction is that intensity. A circle through the point is exactly the cosine.

Move the lamp to 75°. Less light arrives (0.0995, the cosine law again), so the floor is dimmer, but still equally bright from every side: the circle shrinks, and keeps its shape.

The thin blue lines are how the renderer draws a matte bounce: one direction a bounce, crowded near the normal in proportion to the cosine, exactly as the light leaves. A direction drawn that way carries f cos θ divided by its own chance, and for a matte surface that is exactly ρ: every matte bounce multiplies a path's throughput by its albedo and nothing else (chapter 13).

The mathsWhy ρ/π, and why the cosine draw weighs ρ

The light going out towards ωo is the light arriving from every direction ωi, each times f and its cosine, added up over the half-sphere above the point:

Lo(ωo) = ∫ f(ωi, ωo) Li(ωi) cos θi dωi

A matte f is a constant. Lit by a sky of luminance 1 every way, the point gets π (chapter 5's fold-out: the whole half-sphere pressed onto the floor is a disc of area π) and sends back fπ in luminance. That must be ρ of 1, so f = ρ/π. In the flat world the half-circle pressed onto the floor is a line 2 long, so f = ρ/2.

The renderer draws ωi with chance per unit of direction p = cos θ/π. One drawn direction stands for the whole integral as (f cos θ / p) Li (chapter 12), and f cos θ / p = (ρ/π) cos θ / (cos θ/π) = ρ.

A mirror has no such p: all its light goes one way, a direction with chance 1 out of infinitely many. It is called a delta, and it can only be followed, never aimed at.

2A mirror, which nothing can aim through

A room lit by a lamp near its ceiling, rendered as chapter 14 renders it: at every matte bounce a shadow ray aims at a random point of the lamp. The right wall is matte. The strip below is the eye's picture, 56 pixels, its average 0.1320 once 96 paths a pixel are in.

Now the right wall is a mirror reflecting 95% of the light. The eye sees the room again in it, the lamp included: pixel 26 reads 0.950, the lamp's 1 times the mirror's 0.95. No shadow ray found that. At a mirror a path has no choice to make, it goes on along the reflection, and a shadow ray from the mirror to a random point of the lamp would almost never lie along it. So the renderer does not aim at a mirror bounce; the path finds the lamp, or not, by going on. Chapter 15's weights give such a hit a weight of 1, since aiming could not have produced it.

In ShadyTracer a metal's colour is its reflectance straight on, and towards grazing every metal reflects more, up to all of it (Schlick's formula, below). Any surface smoother than a roughness of 0.0316 is treated as a perfect mirror: below that, chapter 9's rough reflection is narrower than anything the march can resolve.

3The white furnace

How do you know a material keeps the light it should? Put it in a white furnace: a sky of brightness 1 in every direction and nothing else. A surface of albedo 1 sends back all it receives, so wherever the eye looks it sees 1, and the shapes vanish. Here two discs, close together so that light bounces between them: every pixel reads exactly 1. Not about 1: every path, through every pixel, brings back 1, since each matte bounce weighs ρ = 1 and every path ends in the sky. Switch to Mirror: the same.

At an albedo of 0.7 they appear: each bounce keeps 0.7, the gap between them, where light bounces more than once, darkest. The picture's average is 0.8128.

At albedo 1 again, but with paths stopped after one bounce: the light that needed a second bounce to escape the gap is lost, and the gap shows (0.9963). A furnace finds a renderer's losses as well as a material's.

And a black pair, albedo 0, as a control: a test that only ever passes might not be looking. ShadyTracer's furnace test has one, after a page that mixed old cached code with new read exactly the sky on every row, rough glass included (section 6).

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A matte floor that was a plastic. makeDiffuse used to carry a 4% shiny coat:
        "a plastic", which "cost 2.5% of its light". Chapter 5's white floor read 0.975 of ρE/π until it
        went.
    • 
      
  • A diffuse term that lost energy. The matte part under a coat was weighted by what the coat
        did not reflect, and the energy a rough coat loses was given back to nobody: a white matte floor "could not pass
        a white furnace" (microfacet.js's header). The base is now coupled to the coat's own reflectance,
        so whatever the coat does not send back, the base gets.
    • 
      
  • A furnace that read the sky. On 2026-09-29 a page mixing cached old code with new read
        1000.000244 with no noise on every row: no sphere had been drawn at all. The test now has a black sphere that
        must read well below 1 (the comment above it in lib/pathtracer/tests.js).
