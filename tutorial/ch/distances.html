Shapes as distances

A ray has to find where it meets a shape. ShadyTracer's shapes are not made of triangles; each one is a formula that answers a single question: how far is it from here to the nearest surface? That answer turns out to be all a ray needs.

1A distance for every point

Take a circle with its middle at c and radius r. How far is a point p from it? Measure the distance to the middle, and take away the radius. That is one subtraction. Drag the probe: the circle drawn around it is as large as it can be without touching the shape. Nothing at all is inside that circle.

Now drag the probe inside. The distance becomes negative: it says how deep the point is. Positive outside, zero exactly on the surface, negative inside. A formula like this is a signed distance field, SDF for short. The thin lines are places with the same distance, like the height lines on a map; the bold one is the surface itself.

Two shapes together? Ask each one and keep the smaller answer: the nearest surface is the nearer of the two. That is a union, and it is one comparison. Cutting one shape out of another, or keeping only where two overlap, are just as short (Building and bending shapes).

Or melt them together: a smooth blend takes a little off the smaller distance wherever the two are close, and a fillet appears between them. This is the very formula ShadyTracer uses, in its flat form.

In 3D the circle becomes a sphere and nothing else changes: |p − c| is then the length in three dimensions. A box takes a few more lines; the help's widget has it in 2D, below.

2Sphere tracing

Here is the trick. A ray starts somewhere and wants to know where it first hits anything. Ask the field for the distance at the start. Nothing is closer than that, in any direction, so the ray can safely move that far along its own direction. At the new point, ask again, move again. Each circle in the figure is one question, and the ray hops from the middle of one to its edge. Near a surface the distances shrink, the hops get shorter, and the ray creeps up to it.

This is sphere tracing (in 3D the circles are spheres). It needs nothing from a shape but its distance. Drag start and aim.

Most rays need only a handful of steps. The expensive ones pass close to a surface without hitting it: every distance is small, so every step is small. Aim the ray just past the corner of the block and count.

3Close enough, and not too far

The distances shrink but never quite reach zero, so the march stops when the distance is below a small number, the surface tolerance. Make it large and rays stop early, short of the surface: edges look soft and thin gaps close up. Make it tiny and rays need more steps to arrive. ShadyTracer chooses it from the scene's size, and never smaller than the computer's numbers can tell apart at that point (Numbers have grain).

Why move only 0.9 of the distance, the step scale? Because a step longer than the real distance can carry the ray straight through a thin wall. Set the step scale above 1 and watch the ray jump the wall: it goes on to hit the ball, a surface it could never have seen. Nothing in the result looks broken. The pixel just shows the wrong thing.

And when there is nothing to hit? A ray that is outside the scene's radius and moving away from the middle can never come back, so the march stops and the ray is given the sky. ShadyTracer asks where the ray is, not how far it has gone (a ray that bounced around inside for a long way has not left). That is called an escape.

4The one rule: never too large

Sphere tracing trusts the distance completely. If a formula ever says the nearest surface is further than it really is, the ray can step through it. Here is an easy way to get that wrong: take the circle's formula and squeeze it sideways to make an ellipse. The shape looks right. But the distances are now too large across the squeezed direction. The red circles claim to be empty and are not, and the ray lands inside.

The fix is to know how much faster than a real distance the formula can change (here: one over the squeeze) and divide by it. The steps get shorter and the ray stops at the surface. A promise that a formula never changes faster than a true distance is a Lipschitz bound. A formula that keeps it is called safe; one that equals the true distance is exact.

6Tricks and trade-offs

    

      
Leaving the scene is a test, not a shape. Every scene used to contain a huge sphere around
        everything, the sky dome, so that a ray going nowhere would still hit something. That sphere was part of every
        distance the scene computed, at every step of every ray. Now the march checks two dot products once a step
        instead; the comment in the excerpt above calls it "strictly cheaper".

    

    

      
Steps of 0.9. The step scale defaults to 0.9
        ():
        a small margin against a formula that is very slightly too large, paid for with somewhat more steps. In the
        march figure, compare the step count at 0.9 and at 1.0 for the same ray.

    

    

      
Longer steps, taken back when wrong. Over-relaxed sphere tracing steps 1.2 to 1.6
        times the distance and steps back when the two circles do not overlap. ShadyTracer does not do this; its
        performance notes list it as an idea whose gain "is not known" here. It would cut steps in open space; what it
        costs near surfaces in these scenes has not been measured.

    

    

      
Normals for free. The same shapes that give a distance also give the direction out of the
        surface, so a hit needs no extra questions to find which way the surface faces. Without that, the renderer
        asks the scene three more times per hit (Which way is up).

    

  






  

    
7What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • The sky dome. Described above: a sphere in every scene, costing a little at every step of
        every ray, replaced by a two-line test.
    • 
      
  • Textbook formulas that broke the rule. A well-known pyramid formula gave distances too large
        inside the pyramid, by up to half its base, in 15 to 46% of the solid. A well-known parabola's sign flipped near
        the curve in the GPU's number format, so the whole axis read as surface. Both were found by measuring every
        shape against an independent reference, and replaced.
    • 
      
  • sign(0) is 0. The GPU's sign function returns 0 for 0, not ±1, which made
        normals of the wrong length on an axis. ShadyTracer's shapes use a comparison instead. Small, and the kind of
        thing only a test finds.
