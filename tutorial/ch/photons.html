Part V · Light the camera can't find · Chapter 19

Photon mapping

Chapter 18 ended on a path neither side could make: a caustic seen through a pane of glass. The camera's shadow rays cannot aim through the ball, and a photon's straight line to the eye meets the pane. A photon map meets the two in the middle: photons are kept where they land, and the eye's path, arriving through the pane, asks how much light landed near it. This chapter is how that works, why it blurs, how the blur is made to fade, and how a GPU finds the photons near a point among millions.

1Light kept where it lands

Chapter 18 in short. A caustic is light that glass or a mirror gathers onto a surface. The camera's paths find the sun at every matte point by aiming a shadow ray at it, but not through glass, which bends the line: through glass they find it only by a lucky bounce. A lucky find counts for a lot, 85 here against the open floor's 0.39, because what a path finds is divided by the chance of finding it, and that chance is small. So light tracing sends photons from the sun through the glass and joins each landing point to the eye, and one rule gives every kind of path exactly one owner, the camera or the photons, so no light is counted twice. A sample is one path through every pixel; the picture is the average of the samples, and ± is its error bar (chapter 12).

Chapter 18's ball and sun, with a pane of glass standing on the floor between the eye and the ball. The strip below is the eye's picture; the pixels that look through the pane are bracketed, and the caustic is among them. Here the picture is made as chapter 18 left it, the camera's paths with light tracing. Light tracing cannot count the caustic through the pane (its line to the eye meets the pane), and the camera's bounces find the sun through the ball only by chance: 9 times in 300 samples over the pane's 14 pixels, each find worth 85. Point at the strip: pixel 24 reads 1.45 ± 0.63, a value as unsure as it is big, and pixel 25 beside it, where the caustic is brightest, 0.03. The readout's error bar over the pane, ±0.060, is that luck. Given enough samples the camera's paths get there (0.324 ± 0.060 is within its error bar of the map's 0.366 below, and the tutorial's test runs both long enough to agree); the map gets there in a few.

Now the photon map. Each sample's photons go first, as in chapter 18, and every one that has passed glass or a mirror is stored where it lands on a surface that scatters: where, how much light, which way the surface faces (the short ticks on the floor, the first sample's 181). Then the camera's paths. One that has met only glass since the eye, here through the pane and onto the floor, gathers there: it adds up the photons stored within a radius r of where it landed, 0.06 here (the dashed circles), and turns them into the light the floor sends back to the eye (the fold-out). The caustic is there from the first sample, and smooth: pixel 25 reads 0.72, and over the pane the pixels average 0.366 ± 0.003 after 300 samples. The path then bounces on, and any light it reaches through glass alone is the map's now, so it is not counted twice: chapter 18's rule of one owner for each kind of path, with the map a third owner.

The price is r. Make it 0.4: the gather now averages the light over 0.8 of floor, and the caustic's peak is spread over its surroundings. Pixel 25 reads 0.48 where it read 0.72. This is a bias, an error that does not average away: every sample makes the same blur. The pane's average hardly changes, though: a blur moves light from the peak to the pixels beside it, mostly inside the pane, rather than losing it. The error is in where the light is.

Make it 0.012, and the blur is gone, but each gather now finds only a photon or two, so every pixel's error bar is about twice as large (point at pixels 25 and 26: ±0.02 where 0.06 gave ±0.01; the pane's average, over 14 pixels, hides most of it). Wide is smooth and wrong, narrow is right and noisy. Section 2 has both.

The mathsWhat a gather adds

A photon carries Φ, its share of the sun's light (chapter 18). The photons that land on a stretch of floor of length ℓ, added up and divided by ℓ, are the light arriving per length there: the floor's irradiance E, measured. The matte floor sends back f × E towards the eye, f = ρ/2 in the flat world (chapter 8). A gather takes ℓ to be the stretch within r of the point, 2r long:

L ≈ f × Σ Φp2r   in the flat world,   L ≈ Σ f Φpπr²   on a surface in 3D

where the sum is over the photons within r, and in 3D f depends on the direction each came from, so it sits inside the sum. What this measures is E averaged over the 2r (or the disc), not E at the point: that average is the blur. A photon whose surface faces more than about 25° away from the gather's is on another surface (a wall meeting the floor) and is not counted.

2A blur that fades

One point of the floor, under the ball where the caustic peaks. Its true brightness is 0.938, found without any randomness: 400,000 of the sun's rays sent evenly across the ball and followed through it exactly (the dashed line). Each sample sends 600 photons and gathers within r₁ = 0.25 of the point; the red curve is the average of the samples so far. It settles, but on 0.623, not on 0.938: on the light averaged over the 0.5 of floor round the point (the dotted line). More samples only make it surer of the wrong value.

Claude Knaus and Matthias Zwicker's way out (2011): each sample is a photon map of its own, so each can have its own radius, smaller every time, by the rule

rk = r₁ × 1 + α2 × 2 + α3 × … × k − 1 + αk

with α between 0 and 1 (the lower panel; α = 2/3 here, ShadyTracer's). The first samples are blurred and the late ones sharp, and the average of them all, the blue curve, climbs towards the true value: 0.905 after 300 samples, the radius down to 0.041. Slowly, because the blurred early samples stay in the average, but the blur's share of it falls towards nothing while the late samples' noise, though larger, is averaged over more and more of them. No pixel has to remember anything but its average, which every pixel keeps anyway (chapter 12): the paper's point. A renderer whose error fades to 0 with more samples, though every finite picture is a little wrong, is called consistent.

α sets the pace. At 0.25 the radius falls to 0.004 by sample 300, the blur goes sooner, and the average reaches 1.002, past the true value: a late sample's gather catches one photon or none, so each sample jumps and the curve jumps with them. ShadyTracer uses α = 2/3, the paper's recommendation. In 3D it is the disc's area πr² that shrinks by the rule, so r² does; on the flat world's line the "area" is 2r, so r itself does.

The mathsWhy the average converges

Two words first. "a ∝ b" says a grows in proportion to b: double one and the other doubles. Variance is the square of the noise, the spread of a sample's value about its mean (chapter 12); averaging k samples of equal variance divides it by k.

noise
with N photons landing on each unit of floor, a gather finds about N × 2rk, give or take its square root (how counts of independent random events spread). The gather divides the count by 2rk, so its spread is √(N 2r) ÷ 2r = √(N ÷ 2r), and its square, the variance, is ∝ 1/rk: fewer photons, more noise, growing as k(1−α). The variance of an average of k independent samples is the sum of their variances divided by k², here about k × k(1−α) ÷ k² = k(−α): it falls, for any α above 0.
blur
a smooth curve averaged over ±r differs from its middle value by about its bend × r²/6, so the error grows as r² ∝ k(2α−2), the table's last column. Its average over k samples falls too, for any α below 1.

So both go, and α trades one against the other: small α kills the blur fast and the noise slowly, large α the reverse. At α = 1 the radius never shrinks (section 2's first step); at 0 it shrinks so fast that the noise never falls.

3Finding the photons near a point

A ShadyTracer sample keeps up to two million photons, and every camera ray seen through glass gathers: for each of the 921,600 pixels of a 1280 × 720 picture to look at every one would be nearly two million million records a sample. So the floor is cut into cells 2r wide (the ticks on it). The photons within r of a point can then lie only in its own cell or in the neighbour on the side of the cell's middle the point is on: two cells (shaded), and in 3D, two on each axis, 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. Most cells hold no photon, so they are not kept as an array but in a hash table: a cell's number times a large constant (73856093, the engine's), and the remainder after dividing by the table's size, picks its list. (The large constant spreads neighbouring cells over the lists. The product is kept as a 32-bit whole number without a sign, so a negative cell's wraps round to a large positive one: cell −3 gives −221,568,279, which wraps to 2³² − 221,568,279 = 4,073,399,017; and since the table's size is a power of two, its remainder is just the product's last bits, here 1: list 1. In 3D a cell is three numbers, each multiplied by a constant of its own and the three combined into one, bit by bit. The page runs the engine's JavaScript, hashCell; its WGSL twin, sppmHash, is the same formula.) Drag the gather point; the blue ticks are the photons it counts, the blue bar its 2r. Here it walked 20 records and counted 10.

Two cells can pick the same list. With four lists (ShadyTracer has a million, so this is rare there), cell −3 shares list 1 with cells 1 and −11, and cell −2 shares list 2 with cell 2 (a list's label names the cells that have photons on it; −3 has none). A gather at −0.5 walks ten records of those other cells (red; one of them far off the figure's left edge) to count the one of its own. Counting every record a list holds would be wrong twice over: a record of cell 1, far away, would count for a gather at −0.5, and a gather whose two cells picked the same list would walk it twice and count its records twice. So each record keeps where it is, and is counted only on the visit to its own cell, and only within r. A collision costs time, never light.

Light piled into a line can put thousands of photons in one cell: 8,315 records in one list, counted on the dichroics scene. A gather walking them all takes that long, and the code bounds a thread's time, so until version 0.61.3 a gather stopped at 512, and the light it did not reach was lost where the photons were densest. Now every record is also put on one of a cell's 32 sub-lists, drawn at random as it is stored, and each list counts its records (all the records on it, whichever cells share it). Past its share of the budget, a gather walks only m of the sub-lists, from a random start, and every record it finds counts 32/m times. Step by step:

  • The budget is the records one gather may walk: 512 in the engine. Shared over the 8 cells it visits, each cell's share is 512 ÷ 8 = 64. This figure uses a share of 6.
  • A cell whose list holds no more than its share is walked whole.
  • One that holds more is walked through m = 32 × share ÷ count of its sub-lists, rounded down: on average that is about a share's worth of records.
  • Here list 1 holds 8 records: m = 32 × 6 ÷ 8 = 24 sub-lists, each find counting 32/24 = 1.33. List 0 holds 12: m = 32 × 6 ÷ 12 = 16, each find counting 2.

From start 0 the gather reads 0.738 against the whole walk's 0.615; press The next start to see the others. Averaged over all 32 starts it reads 0.615 exactly: each record is on the walked sub-lists in m of the 32 starts, and counts 32/m each time. And m is decided by the list's count alone, never by which sub-list a record drew, or the weights would favour some records. Right on average, a little noisier only where it acts.

4How ShadyTracer does it

In outline. Storing: a photon that has passed glass and lands on a surface that scatters takes the next record number (an atomic add on a counter), writes where it is, its light and its two directions there, and puts itself at the front of its cell's list and of one of its 32 sub-lists (an atomic exchange of the list's head). Gathering: a camera ray visits its 8 cells; for each it reads the list's count, walks the whole list or m of its sub-lists, and adds every record that is its own cell's, within r, on a surface facing the same way and within the bounce cap, each times the surface's f; the sum is divided by πr².
