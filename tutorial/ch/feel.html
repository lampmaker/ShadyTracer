Part VII · Speed · Chapter 28

Feeling fast

A renderer can be fast and still feel slow: a camera that lags behind the mouse, a page that does not answer while a heavy picture renders, a frame the graphics card's driver gives up on. This chapter is how ShadyTracer's live view decides how much to draw each frame, what it draws while something moves, how it cuts a heavy picture into pieces that fit a frame, and how an offscreen render keeps the card busy. Most of it is rules in code; where a number was measured, it says so, and where nothing was, it says that.

1How many samples a frame

The live view is a loop: each time the browser is ready to show a new frame, the renderer traces some samples and adds them to the picture. More samples a frame, a quieter picture sooner; but a frame that takes too long makes the view sluggish. A governor decides: it measures the frames a second the loop is managing, and when that is above 23 it asks 3% more samples next frame, below 18, 3% fewer; in between it leaves the number alone. The number is a fraction and the frame traces its whole part. Here each frame costs 5 ms plus 20 ms a sample (a model's numbers): the governor settles at 2 samples, 22 frames a second, and stays.

A light scene, 5 ms a sample: it climbs, 3% a frame, to 8 samples a frame in about 35 frames, and stays. The steps are a share of the count, so they are as gentle at 2 as at 40.

The rule before, as its comment tells it: steps of 0.1 sample, whatever the count, and a band of only one frame a second either side of 20. At 2 samples the frame is 45 ms, 22 a second, over the band, so it climbs; at 3, 65 ms, 15 a second, under it, so it falls: it hunts between the two for ever, and the frame rate with it. "Gentle where it does not matter and violent where it does", the comment says of the fixed step.

And its floor guard, count > 1, let floating point through. Steps of 0.1 do not land on whole numbers in a computer's arithmetic: from 3, nineteen steps down reach 1.0999999999999983, still more than 1, so the guard allows one more, to 0.9999999999999983, whose whole part is 0 (computed). A frame then traced no sample, showing the picture unchanged "while the HUD cheerfully reports 60 fps", in the comment's words. On a heavy scene (60 ms a sample) the old rule here does just that, every other frame. Today's clamps the count at 1.

2Lighter while moving

While the camera, an object or a value is being moved, every frame resets the picture (chapter 23): the view shows one or two samples at a time, all noise, and a full path-traced sample is slow. So while something moves, a full picture is traced in a lighter mode, Draft (light straight from the sun, the sky and the lamps, with shadows, no bounce light) or Sketch (a fixed light), and the full picture comes back once the moving stops. "Moving" is told, never guessed: the camera and the builder's live values poke it each time they change, and it stays on until 400 ms have passed with no poke. Here a drag from 0.3 s to 1.7 s that stands still, button down, from 0.7 to 1.3: the view traces Draft from the first poke until 0.4 s after the button comes up.

A drag that only poked would go back to the full picture in the middle of that pause, at 1.1 s, and to Draft again when the mouse moves on: the full picture flashing under the pointer. So a drag in the picture holds "moving" for as long as the button is down, and the 400 ms start when it is let go.

Why told and not guessed from the picture's resets: a reset is also written by a scene swap, a kernel build, a resize, and by the switch to the lighter mode itself, which would then keep itself on for ever. With fast while moving off, the view traces the full picture throughout. What the lighter mode saves while moving has not been measured.

3A heavy sample in pieces

A sample is every pixel's path. A light scene's sample takes 20 ms: one frame holds it whole, and the governor of section 1 gives the frame more.

A heavy scene's takes a second: as one frame, the view would answer the mouse once a second. So since 0.58.0 the live view cuts a sample into batches, bands of the picture's pixels, and traces about 30 ms of them a frame (the setting sliceMs); the sample is added to the picture when its last batch is done. A second's sample: 34 batches, 34 frames of about 30 ms. The picture changes once a sample, as before; the page answers every frame.

But a batch has a floor: no fewer than 16,384 rays, since a batch that small leaves most of the card idle (the code's comment says inferred). At 1280 × 720 that is 57 batches at most, so a 10-second sample is frames of 175 ms, not 30: slicing cannot hold 30 ms on a very heavy scene. Nothing of this has been seen on a card or measured: the version that brought it says so.

A second rule, older and measured by an accident: Windows resets the graphics card when one piece of work sent to it, one submission, runs about 2 seconds, and the Noise library, over a second a sample as one submission, hung (2026-09-29). So a frame over 250 ms is cut into submissions of about 150 ms each, by iterations (chapter 22). At 640 × 360 the floor allows only 15 batches: a 10-second sample makes frames of 667 ms, each sent as 5 submissions of 5 iterations.

4Keeping the card busy

Offscreen, rendering a picture to save, each sample used to be recorded (the main processor writing the card's list of work), sent, and waited for before the next was recorded. The card idled while the next was recorded, and the processor while the card ran: a sample cost both times added. The benchmark's first runs put a sample's wall clock 50 to 57 ms above its time on the card, of which 6 to 12 ms was the recording.

With two in flight, the next sample is recorded while the card runs this one, and no more than two are ever waiting. A sample then costs about the larger of the two times. Measured on the user's Pascal card: 13 to 25% off a sample (the Noise library 257.6 to 225.0 ms, materials 57.5 to 42.9). Saving a picture, the timeline's frames and the library's pictures ask for two; the benchmark's own samples stay one at a time, so a sample is timed alone.

6Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
Hold the loop, do not stop it. While the builder compiles a changed scene, the live loop is
        held: still running, submitting nothing. A revisit of the Noise library took 1.4 to 6.8 seconds with the loop
        running and 0.2 with it paused (the user's GTX 1080 Ti, 2026-10-03, render-loop.js). Not stopped,
        because a stop inside another (a scene swap's, inside the builder's apply) saw the loop paused and never
        started it again; holds are counted and nest.

    

    

      
Never more than two frames queued. The browser asks for a frame 60 times a second whatever
        the card manages; a loop that sent one each time would build a queue of frames, and that queue is the camera's
        lag (the library this comes from once measured seventeen seconds of it). So the loop waits while one frame is
        queued if a sample, and the frame, take over 50 ms, while two are queued otherwise
        (lib/render-cost.js).

    

    

      
A sample that never finishes. If what a sample was started for changes every frame (a size,
        the scene), the pending sample is dropped; three drops in a row and the next sample runs whole, so the picture
        changes at least that often (raytrace_main.js, NEVER FINISHING).

    

    

      
Slicing's floor. That a batch under 16,384 rays leaves the card mostly idle is the code's own
        inference, and what slicing costs a sample in total is not measured: each batch runs the whole chain of kernels
        (chapter 22), so more batches may mean more fixed cost. What would test it: one heavy sample timed whole and in
        57 batches.

    

    

      
A run that ends on a lost card says so. A benchmark run lost the card at row 19 and sat on
        "running" (the user's Pascal card, 2026-10-04); a watchdog now races each step against the card's loss and a
        timer, keeps the rows done, and names the row it stopped in. The timers are choices, not measurements: a minute
        for a sample, where the slowest seen was 1.35 s (runwatch.js, tests.js).
