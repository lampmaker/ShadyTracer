Building and bending shapes

A die with dots cut into it, a lamp shade melted onto its stand, a twisted column, a rough stone: none of them is a sphere or a box, and all of them are built from spheres and boxes. Each way of combining or changing a shape is a small change to its distance formula. The hard part is keeping the one rule of chapter 2 while doing it: a distance must never be too large.

1Three ways to join two shapes

Chapter 2 joined a circle and a box by keeping the smaller of their two distances: the nearest surface is the nearer of the two. Everything in either shape is in the result, a union. The faint outlines are the two shapes as they were; the bold line is the surface of the result. Drag either shape by the circled cross in its middle.

Keep the larger of the two distances instead, and only what is in both shapes is left: an intersection. A point outside either shape has a positive distance to it, and the larger number says so.

To cut the circle out of the box, turn the circle inside out first: change the sign of its distance, so that inside becomes outside. Then take the larger, as for an intersection: what is in the box and in the circle's outside is left. That is a subtraction. These three, with the shapes themselves, are constructive solid geometry: a die is a rounded box with a sphere subtracted for every dot.

Each of the three can be blended. The smooth blend of chapter 2 takes a little off the smaller distance where the two are within k of each other, and a fillet fills the corner. Its twin for the larger of the two adds a little instead.

A blended subtraction rounds the edges of the cut, where the circle's bite meets the box's sides (in 3D: the rim of a hole drilled into a block). Move the blend k slider to see how far the rounding reaches.

The mathsMin, max and their smooth twins

dtogether = min(a, b),   doverlap = max(a, b),   da minus b = max(a, −b)

h = max(k − |a − b|, 0),   smin = min(a, b) − h24k,   smax = max(a, b) + h24k

a, b
the two shapes' distances at the same point
−b
the second shape turned inside out: negative outside it, positive inside
k
the blend: how close the two distances must be for the blend to change anything. With k = 0 the blend is the plain min or max

Are these still distances? Outside, min of two true distances is the true distance to the nearer shape. Inside, and for max, the result is not always the true distance, but it is never larger than it, which is all chapter 2's rule asks. The blends take off or add at most k/4, and stay safe too: their slope is a mix of the two shapes' slopes (section 2's maths), so it is never steeper than 1, and a formula that is 0 on its surface and never steeper than 1 is never larger than the distance to that surface.

2Which way does a fillet face?

Light bounces off a surface according to which way the surface faces: its normal, the arrow straight out of it. The shapes the scene builder offers mostly give their normal along with their distance (chapter 4 says why that saves work); the plain shapes of the renderer's shape library give none, as the comment in section 6 says of them. A blend has to make one for the fillet, which neither shape has. Here a ball sits on a box, blended. The arrows are normals, and the thick line is the surface lit from the upper left: brighter where its normal faces the lamp. In the blend's zone the arrows are drawn in the accent colour. Every arrow stands square to the surface, and the light runs smoothly over the fillet.

This is how it looked before a bug was fixed. Here the box is one of the shapes that give no normal (an arrow of length zero), like the plain box of ShadyTracer's shape library; the ball gives one. The blend mixed the two arrows and stretched the mix back to length one, and a mix of nothing with the ball's arrow is the ball's arrow, whatever the mix. So the whole fillet was lit as if it were part of the ball: look at the accent arrows on the box's top near the ball, pointing away from the ball's centre instead of straight up.

With a wider blend the wrong zone is wider. The surface itself was always in the right place, since the distances were right; only its light was wrong, so the fillet looked warped, and no smaller step could fix it. The fix: if either shape gives no normal, the blend gives none either, and the renderer works it out from the blended distance itself, which is always right (chapter 4 shows how). That is the Now button.

The mathsThe slope of a blend

A normal is the direction in which the distance grows fastest, its gradient, written ∇. Where a is the nearer shape and the blend is active (h > 0), smin = a − h²/4k with h = k − (b − a). Working out how it changes when a or b changes (a step of calculus; a check: where the two distances are equal, t = ½ and the two normals are mixed half and half) gives

∇ smin = (1 − t) ∇a + t ∇b,   t = h2k

∇a, ∇b
the two shapes' normals
t
how much of b's normal goes into the mix: 0 outside the blend, ½ where the two distances are equal

So the blended normal is exactly a mix of the two normals, mix(∇a, ∇b, t), made one unit long, as long as both exist. With ∇a = 0, the mix is t ∇b, and made one unit long that is ∇b for any t above 0: the bug, in one line.

3Which solid is a point in?

A renderer asks a joined scene two different questions at a point. Which surface is nearest, so a ray knows how far it may step and what it hits? And which solid is the point inside, so a ray travelling through glass or water knows what it is in? (Light bends by how much the two sides of a surface differ, which chapter 10 explains.) Here a glass ball lies in a pool of water. Drag the probe, and the ball. The readout gives both answers; the glass-coloured region is everywhere the renderer would say "in glass".

Why two questions? Here the probe is in the water, just outside the ball: the nearest surface is the ball's, 0.10 away, the one whose distance is smallest in size, inside or out; but the point is in water. The solid a point is in, ShadyTracer decides another way: of all the solids the point is inside (negative distances), the one it is least deep in. A ball lying wholly in the water wins inside itself, always, because no path from a point in the ball reaches the water's surface without crossing the ball's first: the ball's surface is the nearer.

Drag the ball half out of the water. Now the rule is a guess, and here it guesses wrong: the probe is inside the glass, but nearer the water's surface than the ball's, so the renderer says "water". Part of the ball, near the water's edge, has lost its glass. Two solids claiming the same space have no right answer, so build scenes with one inside the other, or cut one out of the other with a subtraction.

4Bending space

To twist a column, ShadyTracer does not change the column. It changes the point the column is asked about: before asking, it turns the point the other way, by an angle that grows with the height. Every slice of the column comes out turned. Changing the point instead of the shape is a warp; the same trick bends, mirrors and repeats shapes. The flat world has no height, so here the angle grows with the distance from the middle instead: a bar, about to become a swirl.

Turned. The shape looks right, but look at the lines of equal distance: on the arms they crowd together. A warp stretches space, and the farther from the middle, the more. A short move across an arm changes the formula by up to 1.77 times what a true distance could change. That breaks chapter 2's rule: the dots mark where it is broken, and the probe's circle, drawn red when it does, claims empty space that is not: it reaches well into the arm.

The fix is chapter 2's: divide by the steepest slope (the formula's Lipschitz bound). ShadyTracer's twist divides by √(1 + c²), with c = the turn rate × the extent, how far the shape reaches from the axis (the dashed circle). That is how much the twist stretches a move straight along the axis (in the flat swirl: straight away from the middle). But some dots stay: a move partly along and partly around is stretched more. The steepest slope left is 1.13, and the probe's circle still cuts a little way into the arm.

The most the map can stretch any move is (c + √(c² + 4)) / 2. Divided by that, no dot is left anywhere inside the extent, the steepest slope is 1.00, and the circle only touches the arm. Both divisors are worked out for the extent: beyond it the swirl stretches more, so the probe stays inside the dashed circle. (Whether the 3D twist's steps are safe beyond its extent is not measured here.)

The mathsHow much a twist stretches

Near a point at distance r from the axis, take two directions: along the axis (up), and around it. A small move up by u turns the point by an extra angle rate × u, and turning a point at distance r by an angle φ moves it r·φ along its circle: so the turned point moves around by c·u, with c = rate × r. A move around by v stays a move around by v. So a move (u, v) becomes (u, v + c u):

|(u, v + c u)|² = u² + (v + c u)²

For a move straight up (v = 0, u = 1) that is 1 + c², so the move is stretched by √(1 + c²). But a move can point any way: write it as u = cos θ, v = sin θ, one unit long, and look for the θ that makes cos²θ + (sin θ + c cos θ)² largest. Trying every θ (or a little algebra) gives the largest stretch:

L = c + √(c² + 4)2

For c = 1 that is 1.618 against √2 = 1.414. The gap is largest near c = 0.7, where L is 1.155 times √(1 + c²). The flat world's swirl stretches by the very same matrix, with r the distance from the middle, which is why the figure shows the twist's numbers.

5Rough surfaces

A rough surface is a shape with a little added to its distance: a noise pattern, smooth hills and valleys of number that are the same every time the same point is asked about. ShadyTracer adds four layers of it, each twice as fine and half as strong as the one before (fBm), and calls it a relief. Here it is on a circle. Added as it is, it makes the formula steeper, as a warp does, and a ray marched through it (the dots are its steps) gets stuck. A march stops when the distance falls below its tolerance (chapter 2); here each step lands just past the surface, the next steps back just short of it, by the same amount, for ever, and the distance never gets small enough. The steps are too close together to tell apart here, so the plot at the right shows the distance the march saw at each of its last ones (its scale changes with the mode, to fit them): a zigzag about zero. After 300 steps it gives up, with half of them inside the shape (red).

Divided by the steepest the noise can make it, the slope bound in the readout, the march is safe and settles, the plot's last steps falling to zero: 33 steps. The bound is 1 + (amount ÷ size) × 17.2 ÷ 1.875: the noise's own steepness (4.3 a layer, section 7, for four layers, over their total strength of 1.875), scaled by how tall the bumps are against how wide. Wider bumps of the same height are gentler. But it pays for the noise at every one of them, even out where the noise cannot possibly matter, and its steps are a fraction of what they could be.

The noise can move the surface by at most a known amount, its reach. So further than twice that from the plain circle (outside the dashed band), the circle's own distance, less the reach, is a safe distance: no noise, no division. Only points inside the band pay for the noise. The same ray: 22 steps.

8What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A hard join was not a number. ShadyTracer's shape library has its own smooth joins
        (), and the scene's joins do not use them,
        for two reasons the comment above csgNormal gives: they divide by k, so a hard join,
        k = 0, gives NaN ("not a number", what 0/0 gives); and they make the mixed normal one unit long even
        when both are zero, which is NaN again. A NaN normal fails the test fold holds every surface to
        (does it face the ray?), and the surface silently disappears.
    • 
      
  • The warped fillet of section 2: the comment's own example is a sphere scooped out of a box,
        the same mix of nothing and the sphere's normal.
    • 
      
  • White dots on a black die. A group used to wear its first shape's material everywhere, and
        switched only for a union's nearer shape. So the dots cut into the die, by charcoal-coloured spheres, had white
        walls. Since version 0.57.2 every part of the surface wears the material of the shape that makes it there, by
        the join's own test (the combine excerpt).
    • 
      
  • A relief divided too early. Divide each shape's distance by c before a blend, and
        the blend comes out c times too wide: smin(x/c, y/c,
        k) is exactly smin(x, y, k c) / c. So a relief inside a
        blend goes in undivided, and the top of the blend divides once, by the steepest relief below it
        (foldRelief).
    • 
      
  • Bumps 1/k too big. A relief sized in millimetres needs the true scale of the space
        it is in. Under a twist, the scale ShadyTracer kept included the twist's factor k (one over its
        divisor), and the bumps came out 1/k times too big; it now keeps the factors apart
