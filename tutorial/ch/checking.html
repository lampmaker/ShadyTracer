Part VIII · Getting it right · Chapter 29

Noise, bias and checking

A picture made of random samples is wrong in two ways at once. Its noise fades as samples add up, and every pixel carries an error bar that says how much is left (chapter 12). Its bias does not fade, and no error bar sees it. This chapter is how to tell the two apart, how ShadyTracer checks itself against answers known in advance, why every such check needs a row that must fail, and how the error bar itself is checked. The checks are in the program; which of them have been run on a real card, and which have not, is said for each.

1Two ways to be wrong

One pixel, looking at a floor in a corner lit by a small lamp near the ceiling. Each sample follows one path from the eye (chapter 13) with no aim at the lamp (no NEE, chapter 14), so the lamp is found only by chance: most paths bring back nothing, a few bring back a lot. The pixel's reading is the average of its samples, and the dashed lines either side of it are two error bars away. Its true value, 1.644, is the long-run average over a million paths (computed). The reading wanders early, then closes in, and the dashed lines hold the true value between them nearly all the way: an unbiased estimate, whose only error is noise.

The same paths, each cut after its first bounce, as a renderer with a bounce cap of 1 would trace them: the light that reaches the floor straight from the lamp, and none that comes off the walls first. The reading settles just as neatly, its error bars close in just as fast, and it settles on 0.94, about 19 of its own error bars under the true value (its own long-run value is 0.926, computed). It is precise and wrong. Nothing in the picture says so: the error bar measures how much the samples disagree with each other, and these all agree on the wrong answer.

A firefly clamp is the other common bias: every sample brighter than a limit is cut to it, here 4, to take the bright speckles out of an early picture. Every path that finds the lamp in one or two bounces brings 7 or 4.9 and is cut, so the reading settles at 1.20, quieter and darker, 17 error bars under (1.183 in the long run). ShadyTracer has one (off by default: "a choice rather than a guard", its comment says), and it clamps only light that has bounced, never what the camera sees directly.

The live view's moving window is not a bias. Averaged over a window of 10 (chapter 23: each new sample weighs 1/11, and old ones fade), the reading follows the true value on average, but it never settles: it is worth about 21 samples however long it runs (section 3), so its error bar stops shrinking. A third kind sits between these: consistent, wrong after any number of samples but less wrong the more there are. The photon map of chapter 19 is one: its radius shrinks with every sample, and so does its blur.

2A check that cannot fail

A check needs an answer known in advance. ShadyTracer's white furnace (chapter 8) is one: a ball of a material that reflects all the light it gets, in a sky that is equally bright every way. No light can be made or lost on the way to the eye, so the whole picture must read the sky's brightness, and each row's ratio (the picture's average over the sky's) must be 1. Here it is in the flat world, one disc filling three quarters of the view, each pixel the average of 32 samples, read the way ShadyTracer's test bench reads its pictures: the ratio, and the noise, how much neighbouring pixels differ, over the average. The white rows pass. A grey disc reads 0.85, and it must: it shows the test can see a loss. The last row is a control: a black disc, which must read well below 1, and does, 0.25.

Now the disc is never drawn: a scene that did not load. Every pixel sees the sky, and every white row reads 1.0000 and passes. They would pass whatever the material did, because an empty picture reads exactly what a perfect one does. The grey row fails, and the control reads 1.0000 too: a row that must fail, passing, is the sign that nothing above it was measured. This happened. On 2026-09-29 a page that mixed old cached files with new ones read 1000.000244 (the sky's brightness, 1000) "with zero noise on every row, rough glass included" (a row that, like the grey here, reads below 1 when it works). The control row's comment tells that story as its reason.

And the camera turned away from the disc: the same readings, from another cause. The first time the haze test was run (the user, 2026-10-16), its camera kept the view's target in the wrong unit, 40 millimetres read as 40 metres, and it looked at nothing but sky: "every row 1000.000244, noise 0, the CONTROL with them". Noise 0 is a clue but not a proof: here the NEE-off row and the mirror read exactly 1 even when the page works, because every one of their samples is exactly 1. The control is the proof.

3Is the error bar right?

Chapter 12 showed how resolve keeps each pixel's error bar from two numbers, the spread V and the sum of squared weights S. The error bar is a claim, and it can be checked: here a thousand pixels, all of the same true brightness 1, each the average of 32 samples drawn as a path tracer's are (mostly small, a few large: an exponential distribution, as the engine's own test of the error bar draws them). Their averages really scatter by 0.175; the error bars they report say 0.176.

256 samples a pixel: both shrink to about 0.065, a third of before, as 1/√n says (√8 is 2.8). The check the engine can run on a real picture uses only that: on a flat, evenly lit floor, two neighbouring pixels differ only by noise, so the average squared difference between neighbours must equal the sum of their squared error bars. Its square root over that is seRatio, and it must read 1: here 1.08, within its own noise (a thousand pixels give about 3% of it, more for a skewed distribution: inferred).

The moving window of 10 (section 1): after 256 samples each new one weighs 1/11, and the average is worth 21 samples, not 10 and not 256. The kernel does not need to be told: S, the sum of squared weights, works its way to 1/21 by itself, and the error bar follows, 0.218 reported against 0.216 real. Why 21 and not 11: the oldest samples still count a little, so the weight is spread over more of them than the window's 10 (the sum of squared weights of a sample weighing w and the rest fading is w/(2 − w), with w = 1/11 that is 1/21).

Now the mistake the engine's comment warns of, "the window's real sample count, which 'N' is not": the error bar worked out as if the window's average were worth its count, 11. It reports 0.308, too wide, and nothing in a picture would look wrong. seRatio sees it: 0.69. The check exists in ShadyTracer, as a row of its test bench, error-bar, with a window-10 row for exactly this; it has been built and never run on a real card, so no seRatio has been recorded anywhere (docs/TESTING.md).

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
A known answer for every kind of light. The bench's image tests each compare a picture with
        arithmetic done in advance: a white floor under the sun and sky reads ρE/π (chapter 5); a tilted
        plane E sin(elevation + tilt); a glowing disc's light on the floor, worked out numerically to match
        the formula; the furnace 1 for every material (lib/pathtracer/tests.js).

    

    

      
A trimmed mean for comparing two builds. Two renderers converging to the same picture can
        differ by percent in a plain mean at a few samples: a handful of fireflies move it. So the bench also reports the
        mean without the brightest 0.1% of pixels; a difference that survives that is real. The NEE test compares plain
        means, because the brightest pixels are exactly what NEE changes (raytrace_main.js,
        docs/TESTING.md).

    

    

      
Noise compared only like with like. The noise number counts real detail in the picture as
        well as noise, so it is compared only between two pictures of the same scene at the same sample count: halving
        it there is worth four times the samples (raytrace_main.js).

    

    

      
The furnace, passed, nearly. Its first run: every material but rough glass 1.000 to 0.04%,
        the NEE-off rows equal to their partners, nothing above 1 (the user's Pascal card, 1188 × 969, 32 samples,
        docs/TESTING.md). Rough glass must read a little under 1, since its model loses the light that
        would bounce between its tiny facets (chapter 9): 0.9587 once fixed (section 6), then 0.9872 and 0.944 in later runs at other picture widths, not
        explained yet. And later runs found the metal rows 0.02 to 0.09% above 1, light made, each about one
        error bar: a bias at the edge of the noise, the largest traced to one table's coarse steps
        (roadmap/SURFACES.md).

    

    

      
The kernels' arithmetic held to a copy. Where the shader's arithmetic matters (the error bar,
        the photon map's gates, the dust's free flight), a JavaScript copy is tested against known answers, and twelve
        *.gpu.mjs tests run the shader's own text on a software graphics card in node to hold the two
        together. Without that software they say SKIPPED, loudly, rather than pass; and they say what a kernel
        computes, never how fast (docs/TESTING.md).

    

    

      
Built is not run. Several of the bench's rows have been built and never run on a card:
        error-bar, render-modes, draft-lamps, the firefly clamp's rows
        (docs/TESTING.md). Until they are, what they check is unknown, and section 3's figure is a model
        of what the error bar should do, not a measurement of what it does. What would settle it: one press of
        error-bar.
