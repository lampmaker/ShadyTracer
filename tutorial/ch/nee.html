Part IV · Path tracing · Chapter 14

Aim at the light

A picture lit by a small lamp comes out full of speckles, and stays that way for a long time. The fix is one extra ray at every bounce, aimed at the lamp on purpose. It is called next event estimation, and it is the most useful single trick in a path tracer.

1The question

This chapter builds on two ideas from earlier ones: a picture is an average of many random samples per pixel (Dice and averages), and each sample follows a path that bounces from surface to surface in random directions until it happens to meet a light (Following a path). Neither chapter is written yet; the figure below shows what you need.

The figure is a small flat room with a lamp near the ceiling, and a camera on the left with one row of pixels. Every pixel sends paths into the room. A path that hits the lamp brings its light back; a path that never does brings back nothing. The first strip under the room is the picture so far. The dots below it are each pixel's value with its error bar: how far off the average may still be. Drag pixel to see the last few paths of a different pixel.

It is speckled, and it settles slowly. The lamp is small, so most paths miss it, and the few that hit it make a pixel much too bright for one sample. That speckle is noise.

2Finding a lamp by chance

Look at a single point on the floor. When a path lands here, a diffuse floor sends it on in a random direction: any direction is possible, those close to straight up a bit more likely. The figure draws 70 such directions. Only the ones that happen to point at the lamp find any light.

Make the lamp small. The lamp gives the same total light (its brightness goes up as it shrinks), but now almost no random direction finds it. When one does, it brings back a lot. The answer is still right on average (look at the estimate in the readout: it agrees with the exact value within its error bar) but each try is either nothing or far too much. That is exactly the speckle in the room.

In ShadyTracerThe sun is the extreme case: a disc only half a degree across, by far the brightest thing in a daylight scene. A random bounce finds it, as the code's comment puts it, "roughly once in two hundred tries".

3Aim at it instead

So don't wait for luck. At every bounce, also pick a random point on the lamp and send a shadow ray to it. If nothing is in the way, add the light that point sends. Every one of these rays is aimed at the lamp, so every one that is not blocked counts. This is next event estimation, NEE: at each bounce, it estimates the light the next bounce could have found, directly.

Compare the two estimates in the readout. Same answer, within the error bars. But the error bar when aiming is many times smaller than when bouncing: far less noise for the same number of tries.

Shadows still work. Put something in the way: the shadow rays that hit it bring back nothing, and the estimate drops to the light that really gets past it.

The mathsWhat a shadow ray adds, and why it divides

Choose a point y on the lamp, at random and evenly along its length ℓ. The chance of each bit of the lamp being chosen is 1/ℓ per unit of length. A Monte Carlo estimate divides what it found by how likely it was to find it (its pdf), so that over many tries the parts it picks often and the parts it picks rarely are counted fairly:

E ≈ Le · cos θx · cos θyr · ℓ   (flat world),    E ≈ Le · cos θx · cos θyr2 · A   (3D)

Le
how bright the lamp is (its radiance)
θx
the angle at the floor point, between its normal and the shadow ray (the cosine law)
θy
the angle at the lamp: a lamp seen edge-on looks smaller
r
the distance between the two points
ℓ, A
the lamp's length, or area in 3D: dividing by the pdf 1/ℓ is multiplying by ℓ

Multiply by the surface's response (for a diffuse surface its albedo over 2 in the flat world, over π in 3D) and by what the path has kept so far, and that is the shadow ray's contribution. If there are several lights and one is chosen with probability p, divide by p as well. The rule is always the same: whatever you chose at random, divide by the chance of choosing it.

4In a whole picture

Back to the room, now traced both ways at once: the same number of paths per pixel, one picture without NEE and one with it. The pictures converge to the same thing (their dots agree within the error bars), but the one with NEE gets there much sooner. The readout gives the average error bar of each.

Shrink the lamp. Without NEE, the picture gets worse and worse: a lamp nothing can find by chance is a lamp the picture never sees. A point light, with no size at all, could never be hit; only NEE could light anything with it. With NEE, the size hardly matters.

Make the lamp large. Now random bounces find it often, and the two pictures are much closer. NEE helps most where light is small and bright, and least where light comes from everywhere.

Don't count it twiceWith NEE on, a bounce that happens to hit the lamp must not add the lamp's light again: the shadow ray already counted it. The flat world simply ignores such hits. ShadyTracer does something better: it keeps both and weighs each by how good it was at finding that light. That is multiple importance sampling, the next chapter.

5Where aiming cannot help

A shadow ray asks a surface: how much of the light coming from that direction do you send to the eye? A matte surface answers "some" for every direction. A perfect mirror answers "none" for every direction except one, the exact reflection. A random point on the lamp is never exactly that direction, so for a mirror or clear glass the shadow ray always brings back zero. Such surfaces are called delta surfaces, and the path has to find the light through them by bouncing.

It gets worse with a lamp behind glass, shining through it onto a floor: the bright patterns under a glass of water. A shadow ray from the floor stops at the glass; a random bounce from the floor has to pass through the glass the exact way that leads to the lamp. These caustics need a completely different approach: tracing light from the lamp's side (Caustics, Photon mapping).

7Tricks and trade-offs

    

      
Shadow rays are not free. On the user's NVIDIA Pascal card (Chrome 154, Windows, 1280 × 720,
        ShadyTracer 0.9.2), the Noise library scene took 184.7 ms of GPU time a sample with NEE and 136.9 ms without:
        the shadow rays were about a quarter of the GPU's work (−26%), almost all of it in the occlusion kernel, 45.5 ms
        to 0.1 ms. One run on one machine, recorded in docs/PERFORMANCE_REVIEW.md §10.1. What that
        quarter buys is the noise you saw in the room: NEE stays on.

    

    

      
The sky counts as a light too. Half of the environment's shadow rays go to the sun's disc and
        half to the sky, spread like a cosine. The comment records why: with shadow rays for the sun alone, the noise
        fell 36% with the sun turned up but only 4% at the default, "because at the default most of the light in the
        scene is sky, and nothing was sampling it". (The comment gives no hardware for that measurement.)
        

    

    

      
Memory only when used. The shadow-ray queue is two more ray-sized buffers, about 200 MB at
        1024 × 1024. They are allocated the first time NEE actually runs, and stay one element each while it is off
        ().

    

    

      
Not on the last round. The GPU processes rays in rounds (Wavefront path
        tracing). A shadow ray made in the very last round would never be traced, so none is made there; the surface
        falls back to finding light by bouncing, noisier but still right on average
        ().

    

  






  

    
8What went wrong before

    
Two edges for one sun. To divide by its probability, a shadow ray to the sun needs to know whether
      a direction is inside the sun's disc. The light itself had its own test for the same question. The two tests were
      written differently and disagreed on a thin ring at the disc's edge, where rounding decides. A shadow ray landing
      in that ring carried the full brightness of the sun divided by the probability of hitting sky:

    

    
The result was a floor 142 times too bright on average, while its typical (median) pixel looked right: a
      few pixels were enormous. It was found because a test compared the floor with a number known in advance.

    
A comparison that did not compare. When NEE was first written, it could only aim at the sun and
      the sky. A lamp made of glowing geometry was invisible to it, found only by bouncing. So an NEE-on against NEE-off
      comparison run in a scene lit by such a lamp measured nothing about NEE at all
      ().
      The fix was to make NEE aim at those lights too. The lesson: before believing a measurement, check that the
      thing being switched actually reaches what is measured.
