Part II · What light is · Chapter 5

Measuring light

So far a ray has brought back "light" as a bare number. ShadyTracer's numbers are real units: a lamp in candela per square metre, the sun in lux, every pixel in candela per square metre, so that a picture can be checked against a light meter, and a floor in the sun against a line of arithmetic. This chapter is those units, the two laws that connect them, and the sun.

1Four ways to say how bright

"How bright is this lamp?" has four answers, each a different question, and lighting uses all four. Each counts light the way the eye does: green light counts for more than blue of the same power, because the eye is more sensitive to it (chapter 6 says how much).

  • How much light it gives off in all: its flux, in lumens (lm). A household LED bulb gives about 800.
  • How much it sends one way: its intensity, in candela (cd): lumens per unit of solid angle, the steradian. A lamp of 1 candela in every direction gives 4π lumens in all, since the whole sphere of directions is 4π steradians.
  • How much lands on a surface: its illuminance, in lux (lx): lumens per square metre. A light meter on a desk measures this.
  • How bright a surface looks: its luminance, in candela per square metre (cd/m²): the intensity each square metre of it sends towards you, counting only the area you see (a tile seen at a slant looks smaller). A camera's pixel measures this.

Light lands as illuminance; anything seen, a lamp or a lit wall, has a luminance. A camera ray brings back luminance, so a pixel holds candela per square metre. The figure is a glowing panel, 0.5 m square, of luminance 1000 cd/m², 2 m from the eye. The eye's picture is 60 × 40 pixels. Every pixel wholly on the panel reads 1000; a pixel at its edge reads its share, the average of the many rays through it of which some miss (chapter 1's jitter). Point at the picture to read a pixel. Add up what each pixel brings the eye (its reading, times the small solid angle it covers, times the cosine of its direction from straight ahead, chapter 1's slant) and the panel puts 61.2 lux on the eye.

Drag the panel twice as far, to 4 m. A pixel wholly on it still reads 1000: the panel looks exactly as bright. What changes is how much of the picture it covers: counting each pixel at its edge by the share it sees, 348.2 pixels' worth at 2 m and 87.1 at 4 m, a quarter. With it the light it brings the eye falls to a quarter too: 15.5 lux. (The readout gives both: the pixels wholly or partly on the panel, and the area they add up to.) That is the inverse square law of chapter 0, and it lives in how much of the view the panel fills, not in the light along a ray. Along a ray, luminance does not change: a ray is one line of sight, and what it sees looks as bright from near as from far.

At 8 m the panel is small beside its distance, and the lux follows the simplest formula: the panel's intensity is its luminance times its area, I = 1000 × 0.25 = 250 cd, and E = I/d² = 3.91 lux. The pixels' sum says 3.90; the exact answer for a square this size is 3.90.

This is why a path tracer carries luminance (radiance, in the physicist's units) along its rays: it is the one quantity a ray does not need to change as it travels. Nor does it care how big the scene is: a room twice as big, seen from twice as far, looks exactly the same, pixel for pixel, so a room built in millimetres and the same room in metres give the same picture, which section 5 shows measured.

One quantity holds the rest together, and the next two sections lean on it: how much light a bright patch of sky (or a lamp, or the sun) puts on a floor. The fold-out works it out; its two answers are E = πL for a whole sky of luminance L, and E = πL sin² α for a round lamp straight overhead whose edge is α from its middle.

The mathsThe four quantities, and light from a patch of sky
Φ
flux, lumens: all the light, counted by the eye's sensitivity
I
intensity, candela: flux per steradian, in one direction
E
illuminance, lux: flux per square metre arriving
L
luminance, cd/m²: intensity per square metre of the surface as seen from that direction, its area times the cosine of the angle from its normal

A small lamp of intensity I, d away, its light arriving at an angle θ from the surface's normal:

E = I cos θd2

A large one, or the sky: picture a glass dome of radius 1 over a point of the floor, the bright patches of sky painted on it. Shine a light straight down from above the dome, and each patch casts a shadow on the floor's disc of radius 1 under it. A patch of luminance L lights the point by L times the area of its shadow. Why: a small patch of the dome, of area Ω, is Ω of solid angle (the dome has radius 1), so it would put LΩ on a surface facing it; the floor meets its light at a slant θ from straight up, and the cosine law takes that down to LΩ cos θ. And a small patch tilted by θ from facing straight down casts a shadow cos θ as big: Ω cos θ. The two match. (This is Nusselt's way of seeing it.) Two cases follow:

  • A whole sky of luminance L presses down onto the whole disc, of area π: E = πL.
  • A round lamp straight overhead, of angular radius α (the angle from its middle to its edge), presses down onto a circle of radius sin α (its edge is a ring on the dome α from the top, at height cos α and radius sin α): E = π L sin² α. For a lamp at a slant, the same illuminance falls on a surface turned to face it, and a level floor gets cos θ of it.

Many renderers write a small lamp's illuminance as L times its solid angle instead: the usual shortcut. The solid angle of a round lamp is 2π(1 − cos α) (the area of that cap of the unit sphere, a fact of geometry), which for a small α is very nearly π sin² α, so the shortcut is close; since sin² α = (1 − cos α)(1 + cos α), it is off by (1 − cos α)/(1 + cos α) of the answer, about α²/4 (for small α, cos α is close to 1 − α²/2) (α in radians: the angle measured as the length of arc it cuts from a circle of radius 1, so 180° is π).

2The sun, in two numbers

ShadyTracer's sun is set by two numbers: the illuminance it puts on a surface facing it, 100,000 lux by default, and how wide its disc looks, 0.53° (). Its luminance follows from those two: E = πL sin² α (section 1: the disc's shadow on the floor's unit disc is a circle of radius sin α), with α half of 0.53°, 0.00463 radians (an angle measured as the arc it cuts from a circle of radius 1: 180° is π), gives L = 1.488 billion cd/m². The figure draws the disc at its true size over a point of the ground, and on the right its luminance for every width, at the same 100,000 lux.

That luminance is a problem for a path tracer. The disc is 0.0000672 steradians, a speck of the sky. A ray bouncing off a matte ground goes in a random direction, more often near straight up (chapter 8 shows how: the chance of a direction goes as the cosine of its angle from straight up, divided by π so that the chances add up to 1), and it finds the disc with the chance 0.0000672 × cos 45° / π, 1 time in 66,111 when the sun is 45° up (45° from straight up as well as from the ground). A few rays that find it bring back a billion; the rest bring back nothing. That is why chapter 14 aims at the sun instead of waiting to hit it.

Widen the disc to 10° across. The illuminance is held at 100,000 lux, so its luminance drops to 4.19 million, and a random bounce finds it 1 time in 186. A bigger sun is a softer shadow, not a brighter picture: the setting's comment says just that. The shortcut L × solid angle is now 0.19% off the exact answer, where at the real sun it was 0.0005%; ShadyTracer uses the exact one at every size.

Small as it is, the real sun's disc is a test of arithmetic. Its 1 − cos α is 0.0000107. The graphics card computes with 32-bit numbers (f32), and near 1 those are kept in steps of about 0.00000006, so cos α is about 180 steps below 1, and 1 − cos α comes out with an error near half a percent. That number is the disc's solid angle (the fold-out), and when the renderer aims a ray at the sun (chapter 14) it says how likely each direction in the disc is; the luminance needs sin² α, as small. So ShadyTracer works both out once, in the program on the main processor, in 64-bit numbers, and hands them to the graphics card (section 4).

3A white floor, measured

Now everything at once, and checked on a real graphics card. A floor of albedo 0.8, matte (it sends its light out evenly in every direction, so it looks equally bright from anywhere), under the default sun at 45° and the default sky. The sun's 100,000 lux arrive at a slant: on the level floor they give 100,000 × sin 45° = 70,711 lux (the cosine law, with the angle measured from the floor up). The sky adds 15,000, its setting being the illuminance it alone puts on a level surface. In all, 85,711 lux.

The floor sends back 0.8 of it (its albedo, written ρ). A matte surface of luminance L sends out πL lux's worth (section 1's sky, turned round: the floor is a sky for the space above it), so L = ρE/π = 0.8 × 85,711 / π = 21,826 cd/m². ShadyTracer's own test of this rendered the floor on the user's NVIDIA Pascal card, 1188 × 969 pixels, 32 samples a pixel: 21,827.2. The ratio is 1.0001.

The sun alone at 20°: sin 20° × 100,000 = 34,202 lux, so 8,709.5 cd/m², and the card measured 8,709.3. Every row of that test is within 0.07% of its arithmetic, except one with aiming switched off (section 5).

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • No units at all. The sun was an "intensity" of 1, its disc 0.1 radians from middle to edge
        (21 times the real sun's), the sky a multiplier on a sky made to look like the one before it, and glowing
        materials were 5: each number was set against the one before it, nothing could be measured, and the sun gave 3%
        of the sky's light (the comment above the sun's settings in uniforms.schema.js, and
        photometry.js's header).
    • 
      
  • Two edges to one sun. The first run of section 3's test found the rows with the real sun at
        142 to 352 times the right mean, with their median right (docs/TESTING.md, "Physical units: the
        first absolute check", Pascal). A path reaches the sun two ways: by aiming a ray at it (chapter 14), and by a
        bounce that happens to go there. Each asks whether a direction is inside the disc, and they asked differently:
        the aiming by dot(L, S) ≥ cos α, the bounce by section 4's cross product. Near the sun the cosine is about 180
        steps below 1, so the two disagreed on a thin ring at the rim. A bounce into that ring was taken as one the
        aiming could never make, so it counted the whole disc's luminance divided by a bounce's tiny chance of finding
        the sun (a rare find counts for more: chapter 8), about 10⁵ too much, in a few pixels. Since then there is one
        test, inSunDisc, and the other was deleted so a second edge cannot come back.
    • 
      
  • A matte floor that was not quite matte. After that fix every row read 0.975, sun and sky,
        metres and millimetres alike. The light was right; the floor was not: its material sent 4% of its light off as
        a shine and reflected like an albedo of about 0.78. With a pure matte floor, the third run's 1.0001.
    • 
      
  • A soft edge that lost light. The sun's disc used to fade over its outer 2%, which every way of
        reaching it saw alike but which took about 1% off its illuminance: a number that is a setting now, and has to
        come out as set (the comment above sunDiscRadiance in sdf.js).
    • 
      
  • A sun under the floor. Below the horizon the sun used to keep shining, through the ground of
        any scene that had none modelled (the comment in derive).
