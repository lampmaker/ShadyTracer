Rainbows

Glass bends blue light a little more than red, so a prism fans white light into a rainbow. A renderer that carries three numbers a ray (chapter 6) has no blue and red to bend apart. This chapter is how ShadyTracer does it anyway: one wavelength drawn for a ray at the moment it enters such glass, and a weight for each of its three channels chosen so that, on average, the colours add back to white; and how a coating that reflects some colours and passes others needs no wavelength at all.

1A prism

The index of refraction of glass is not one number: it is larger for shorter wavelengths, so blue bends more than red. That is dispersion. Here a dense flint glass, of index 1.75 at yellow, fans a beam of white light: each line is one wavelength, 400 to 700 nm, in its own colour, refracted at its own index both into the prism and out. Blue (486 nm) leaves 3.24° more steeply than red (656 nm).

BK7, the commonest optical glass, disperses far less: 0.71°. A glass catalogue says how much by its Abbe number, 28 for the flint and 64.17 for BK7: the higher, the less.

With no dispersion every wavelength bends alike, and the white beam stays white: what ShadyTracer does for a glass whose Abbe number is left at 0, the default. Drag the beam's handle to tilt it; tilt it too far, and some colours are totally reflected inside (chapter 10).

2Two numbers for a glass

A catalogue gives a glass's index at three wavelengths named after dark lines in the sun's spectrum (Fraunhofer's): F at 486 nm, d at 588 and C at 656. It lists nd, and the Abbe number Vd = (nd − 1) / (nF − nC). ShadyTracer takes those two and draws the index at every wavelength with Cauchy's formula, n = A + B/λ²: exactly nd at d, exactly the catalogue's F − C, and Cauchy's shape between. For BK7 it gives nF 1.52243 and nC 1.51438, against the catalogue's 1.52238 and 1.51432 (the rings).

3A wavelength for a ray

A ray carries red, green and blue. When one is refracted into a dispersive glass, the renderer draws it a single wavelength, which it then keeps, out of the prism and into any glass after. The wavelengths are split into 40 bins of 10 nm, and a bin is drawn as often as the three channels together respond to it (the bars, in each bin's colour): yellow-green often, deep violet and deep red rarely. Then each channel is weighted by how much it responds to that wavelength, divided by the bin's chance: point at a bin to read its three weights.

That division is chapter 12's rule again, and it makes each channel's weight average exactly 1 over the draws: a white ray splits into one colour per path, and averaged over paths is white again. 20,000 draws average 0.993, 1.014 and 0.993. A reflection off the prism is decided before any wavelength is drawn, so it stays white and free of colour noise; only the refracted light carries the rainbow, and its noise.

The fit's red end. The colour-matching curves are a fit (chapter 6), and its tails are its weakest part: past about 700 nm its ȳ outlasts its x̄, and its red response is 0 from 733 nm, so the last bins weigh green only (worked out from spectral.js in node). The CIE's own deep reds all look red (the red corner of chapter 6's diagram), so this is the fit's, not the light's. The draw lands there about once in 80,000 (1.25 × 10⁻⁵), so no picture should show it; a test of a deep-red laser through a prism would.

The mathsWhy the weights average to 1

Let Wc(k) be channel c's response in bin k, each channel's responses adding up to 1 over the bins. The bin is drawn with chance P(k) = (WR + WG + WB)/3 and gives channel c the weight Wc(k)/P(k). Averaged over the draw:

Σk P(k) × Wc(k)P(k) = Σk Wc(k) = 1

A weight is at most 3, where one channel alone responds. Drawing in proportion to the summed responses keeps the weights that small, and wastes no draw on a wavelength no channel sees.

4A coating that sorts colours

A dichroic coating, thin layers on glass, reflects some wavelengths and passes the rest: this one reflects above 600 nm, so it reflects red and passes cyan. It needs no wavelength drawn. Each of the ray's three channels already stands for a spread of wavelengths (chapter 6's curves), so the renderer averages the coating's reflectance over each channel's own wavelengths and reflects that much of the channel: red 0.584, green 0.014, blue 0.000. Exact for a coating that reflects the same at every wavelength, an approximation at an edge.

Tilt the light, and the edge moves towards blue, as real coatings do: at 45° it is at 551.8 nm, and now most of the red and a third of the green reflect. The renderer moves it by the usual first-order rule, edge × √(1 − (sin θ / neff)²), with neff the layers' average index, 1.8 by default.

7What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • Green reflections. The optics view drew each ray's colour scaled to its brightest channel,
        right for a white ray and nothing else: a dispersed bundle summed to (0.35, 0.71, 0.14), and the prism's
        reflections, where the colours overlap again, came out green (the user's picture, 2026-10-09). A line is now the
        ray's own red, green and blue, and the same bundle sums to white (CHANGELOG.md, 0.44.1).
    • 
      
  • A wavelength squeezed. The tag first gave the wavelength 16 bits; another flag took one in
        0.44.0, leaving 15, steps of 0.012 nm instead of 0.006 (spectral.js).
    • 
      
  • A test that crashed the driver. A loop around the shading function made the software graphics
        driver crash; the test calls it once a thread instead (dispersion.gpu.mjs).
