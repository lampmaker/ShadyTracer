Part I · Seeing with rays · Chapter 1

Rays make a picture

A computer picture is a grid of coloured dots. To colour one dot, ShadyTracer asks one question: if I look out through this dot, what do I see? This chapter is about how it asks.

1The question

You see the ball because light from the lamp landed on it, and some of that light bounced off into your eye. A camera works the same way. A picture is a record of how much light arrived from each direction, one pixel per direction.

So to make a picture, a program needs to know, for every pixel: what is out there in that pixel's direction, and how much light is it sending back?

A flat worldEvery figure in this tutorial is drawn in a slice of the world, seen from the side: a flat world. The ideas are the same as in ShadyTracer's 3D scenes, and they are much easier to draw. In the flat world a picture is a single row of pixels instead of a grid. Where a formula changes between flat and 3D, the page says so.

Each line from the eye in the figure is a ray: a starting point and a direction. It stops at the first thing it meets, and its pixel takes that thing's colour: brighter where the lamp shines on it, dark on the faces turned away from the lamp (section 5 is why). A ray that meets nothing shows the sky, the pale blue.

The strip at the top is the picture the eye would see. It is the screen turned on its side, so its left end is the top of the screen. Drag the eye, the lamp, the ball and the block by their circled crosses (⊕), and watch the picture change.

2Follow the light, or follow the eye?

The obvious way to simulate light is to do what light does: send rays out of the lamp in every direction and see which ones end up in the eye. Switch the figure to From the lamp. A ray that meets something bounces off it in a new direction. A matte surface like these scatters light every way, not neatly like a mirror (Diffuse and mirror), so each bounce here goes in a random direction. Of every hundred rays, only one or two pass through the eye's pupil, and the pupil is drawn far bigger than a real one. Everything else was work for nothing. The picture stays empty: a pixel needs many rays to arrive before it has a value, and almost none do.

So ray tracers run it backwards. Start at the eye, send a ray out through each pixel, and see what it hits. Every one of these rays is useful, because every one belongs to a pixel. This works because light paths can be walked in either direction: if light can go from the lamp, off the ball, into the eye, then a ray from the eye can find the ball and then the lamp.

The ray from the eye is called a camera ray (or primary ray). It is where every picture ShadyTracer makes begins. How a ray finds the first thing in its way is the whole of the next chapter (Shapes as distances); for now, take it that it can.

Not the whole storySome light really is easier to find from the lamp's side: light focused through glass onto a floor. ShadyTracer does trace some rays from its lights, for exactly that (Caustics, Photon mapping). But the picture starts at the eye.

3One ray per pixel

Put a screen a little way in front of the eye and divide it into pixels. Each pixel's ray starts at the eye and goes through the middle of its pixel. More pixels means more rays, finer detail and more work: the picture's resolution. Move the pixels slider.

How wide the screen is, compared with how far away it is, sets the field of view: how much of the world fits in the picture. A wide field of view fits more in, and stretches what is near the ends of the picture: the screen is flat, so a pixel near its end sees a narrower slice of angle than one in the middle, and anything there covers more pixels.

All the rays start at one point, the eye. That is a pinhole camera: everything is sharp, near and far. A real lens blurs what is out of focus; ShadyTracer can do that too (The camera's lens).

The mathsThe direction of a pixel's ray

Number the pixels across the screen from 0, and give each one a position x from −1 at one end of the screen to +1 at the other (a place on the screen, not a coordinate of the room). Pixel i of N, at a point u inside it (½ is its middle), sits at

x = 2 · i + uN − 1

so pixel 0's middle is just inside −1 and pixel N−1's just inside +1. The camera has a forward direction (where it looks) and a right direction across the screen, each an arrow one unit long. The ray's direction is forward plus a bit of right, then made one unit long:

d = normalise( forward + right · x · tan(fov2) )

tan(fov/2)
how far the screen reaches either side, if it is one unit in front of the eye: the ray to the screen's end makes the angle fov/2 with forward, and tan is the side across over the side along
normalise
divide an arrow by its length, √(a² + b²) for an arrow (a, b), so it is one unit long and points the same way

In 3D there is also an up direction and a second position y for the pixel's row: d = normalise(forward + right · x · fx + up · y · fy), where fx and fy are the half-widths of the screen. That is the line you will find in ShadyTracer's code below.

4In 3D: a grid, not a row

The flat world has a row of pixels. A real picture has rows of them, so in 3D the screen becomes a grid, and the eye sends one ray through each cell. This figure is three-dimensional: drag the background to turn it and look at the rays from the side. Each cell of the screen is painted with what its ray found, and laid flat the cells are the picture in the corner.

Switch on Every ray to see all of them at once: a fan spreading out from one point, the eye, through the grid. Drag the circled cross on the screen to follow one pixel's ray, and change pixels across: the picture gets finer, and the number of rays grows with the square of it. Double the pixels across and down, and there are four times as many rays.

Everything else in this chapter is the same in 3D. The figures stay flat wherever a slice shows the idea more clearly, and turn three-dimensional where the third dimension is the point.

5What does the ray see?

When a ray hits something, the pixel needs to know how bright that point is. The simplest answer asks one more question: can this point see the lamp? Here the chosen pixel's ray meets the ball. Send a second ray from that point towards the lamp. If it gets there without hitting anything, the point is lit. This second ray is a shadow ray. The arrow standing straight out of the surface is its normal, and θ is the angle between it and the lamp's direction.

Now a pixel near the bottom of the screen: its ray meets the floor behind the block. The shadow ray from there is stopped by the block, crossed out where it is blocked. The lamp cannot see this point, so it is in shadow. A face turned away from the lamp is in shadow too: the lamp is behind it. Drag this pixel along the screen to try others.

How much light a lit point gets depends on two things. Distance: further from the lamp, less light. And angle: light arriving at a slant spreads over more surface, so it lights each bit of it less. The next figure shows why.

6Light at a slant

Here is a beam of light w wide, from a lamp far enough away that its rays are parallel. Straight down onto the floor, it lights a patch w long.

Tip the beam (drag the lamp, or the slider). The same beam, with the same light in it, now lands on a longer patch: w / cos θ. The same light shared over more floor gives each bit of it less: cos θ as much. At 60°, cos θ is ½, so each bit gets half. That is the cosine law, and it is why the parts of the ball that face the lamp are the brightest.

The mathsHow bright a lit point is

A lamp of intensity I (how strongly it shines in the point's direction), at distance d from the point, seen at angle θ from the normal, gives the point this much light, its irradiance E:

E = I · cos θd   in the flat world,   E = I · cos θd2   in 3D

The cos θ is the figure's: the light a beam brings, shared over a patch 1 / cos θ times longer. The d is the same idea at a larger scale. In 3D, light from a lamp spreads over a sphere whose area grows with d²; in a flat world it spreads around a circle whose length grows with d. The figures use the flat world's version, because that is the world they draw.

A surface's colour is three numbers between 0 and 1: how much of the red, the green and the blue light falling on it it sends back. The pixel is each of them times E. Measuring light explains the units ShadyTracer gives these numbers.

Rays, a hit, a shadow ray to the lamp, the cosine law: that is already a working ray tracer, and the oldest kind. Its pictures look hard and dark, because it only counts light that comes straight from the lamp. A real room is lit just as much by light bouncing off its walls. The rest of this tutorial is about counting that light too (Following a path), and chapter 14 shows why the shadow ray is still the most useful trick in the whole renderer (Aim at the light).

7Edges, and jitter

A pixel covers a small wedge of directions, not one direction. But a ray through its middle looks in one direction only, and sees either the ball or the sky, never a bit of both. In the figure the ray goes through the middle every time: however many times you try, the pixel reads 0 or 1. In a picture with rows and columns, a slanted edge then flips whole pixels from ball to sky, a row at a time, and comes out as stair steps: aliasing.

Now turn on Jitter. Each try (each sample) sends its ray through a random point inside the pixel. Some hit the ball and some miss, and the average of all of them settles on the share of the pixel the ball really covers. It wobbles on its way and takes many samples to get close: after 35 it can still be well off, after 400 it is near. The edge comes out smooth.

ShadyTracer jitters every camera ray, every sample. That is why its picture starts speckled and gets better while you watch: each frame adds more samples to each pixel's average. The speckle is called noise. How fast an average settles, and how ShadyTracer knows how settled it is, is Dice and averages.

Move the edge slider to slide the ball's edge across the pixel, and press Start again.

9Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each of these is stamped with how it is known: read in code means the code does it and the page links the
      line. Point at a stamp for what the others mean.

    

      
One ray per pixel per sample, then average. Instead of tracing a perfect pixel once, ShadyTracer
        traces a rough one many times and keeps the average. The picture is usable after one sample and better after
        each one, and the camera can move at any time: the average just starts again.

    

    

      
The kernel that makes camera rays does not know the scene. It never reads the shapes, so its
        program text is the same for every scene. Before the GPU can run a kernel it has to
        compile it, translate it into the card's own instructions, which can take seconds; the
        compiled copy is kept and reused when you switch scenes
        ().
        Faster compiles is about this idea.

    

    

      
The lens costs nothing when it is off. With a lens radius of 0 the kernel makes exactly the
        pinhole's ray, and draws no extra random numbers

10What went wrong before

    
Before any ray is made, ShadyTracer decides how to lay a sample's rays over the picture: as a grid of tiles,
      tilesX across and tilesY down, one ray in each. Each ray goes through a random point of
      its tile and counts for whichever pixel that point lands in. A sample has N rays, and for a picture
      w pixels wide and h high there is one ray a pixel, so N = w ×
      h. To give the grid the picture's shape, the number across is √(N × w /
      h), which with N = w × h is √(w × w) =
      w: one tile per column of pixels.

    
The code rounded that number down. A computer keeps a number like w / h with a limited
      number of digits, so it is stored a tiny bit off, and the product can land a hair under the exact answer. For a
      window 809 pixels wide and 395 high, it comes out as 654,480.9999999999 instead of 809² = 654,481; its square root
      is 808.9999999999999, and rounded down that is 808 tiles across for 809 pixels. (Type
      Math.sqrt(809 * 395 * (809 / 395)) into a browser's console to see it.) The tiles were then a little
      wider than pixels, and across the picture they slid further and further off the pixel grid, so some pixels got
      two rays' points in a sample and some none. It happened for about one window size in forty:

    

    
The picture looked fine: over many samples the jitter still covered every pixel. What was wrong was the noise.
      A pixel that gets two rays a sample settles faster than one that gets one, so how speckled a picture was depended
      on the window's size, which nobody comparing the noise of two pictures would think to keep the same. Rounding
      instead of rounding down gives exactly one ray per pixel at every size. The comment says what happened, because the
      next person to touch the line needs to know why it is written that way.
