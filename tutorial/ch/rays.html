Part I · Seeing with rays · Chapter 1

Rays make a picture

A computer picture is a grid of coloured dots. To colour one dot, ShadyTracer asks one question: if I look out through this dot, what do I see? This chapter is about how it asks.

1The question

You see the ball because light from the lamp landed on it, and some of that light bounced off into your eye. A camera works the same way. A picture is a record of how much light arrived from each direction, one pixel per direction.

So to make a picture, a program needs to know, for every pixel: what is out there in that pixel's direction, and how much light is it sending back?

A flat worldEvery figure in this tutorial is drawn in a slice of the world, seen from the side: a flat world. The ideas are the same as in ShadyTracer's 3D scenes, and they are much easier to draw. In the flat world a picture is a single row of pixels instead of a grid. Where a formula changes between flat and 3D, the page says so.

Drag the eye and the lamp in the figure, and the ball and the block by the crosses in their middles. The row at the top is the picture the eye would see.

2Follow the light, or follow the eye?

The obvious way to simulate light is to do what light does: send rays out of the lamp in every direction, let them bounce, and see which ones end up in the eye. Switch the figure to From the lamp. Of every few hundred rays, only one or two happen to pass through the eye's pupil. Everything else was work for nothing.

So ray tracers run it backwards. Start at the eye, send a ray out through each pixel, and see what it hits. Every one of these rays is useful, because every one belongs to a pixel. This works because light paths can be walked in either direction: if light can go from the lamp, off the ball, into the eye, then a ray from the eye can find the ball and then the lamp.

The ray from the eye is called a camera ray (or primary ray). It is where every picture ShadyTracer makes begins.

Not the whole storySome light really is easier to find from the lamp's side: light focused through glass onto a floor. ShadyTracer does trace some rays from its lights, for exactly that (Caustics, Photon mapping). But the picture starts at the eye.

3One ray per pixel

Put a screen a little way in front of the eye and divide it into pixels. Each pixel's ray starts at the eye and goes through the middle of its pixel. More pixels means more rays, finer detail and more work: the picture's resolution. Move the pixels slider.

How wide the screen is, compared with how far away it is, sets the field of view: how much of the world fits in the picture. A wide field of view fits more in and makes things near the edges look stretched.

All the rays start at one point, the eye. That is a pinhole camera: everything is sharp, near and far. A real lens blurs what is out of focus; ShadyTracer can do that too (The camera's lens).

The mathsThe direction of a pixel's ray

Number the pixels across the screen and give each one a position x from −1 at one edge to +1 at the other. Pixel i of N, at a point u inside it (½ is its middle), sits at

x = 2 · i + uN − 1

The camera has a forward direction (where it looks) and a right direction across the screen. The ray's direction is forward plus a bit of right, then made one unit long:

d = normalise( forward + right · x · tan(fov2) )

tan(fov/2)
how far across the screen reaches, for a screen one unit in front of the eye
normalise
scale a direction to length 1, keeping where it points

In 3D there is also an up direction and a second position y for the pixel's row: d = normalise(forward + right · x · fx + up · y · fy), where fx and fy are the half-widths of the screen. That is the line you will find in ShadyTracer's code below.

4In 3D: a grid, not a row

The flat world has a row of pixels. A real picture has rows of them, so in 3D the screen becomes a grid, and the eye sends one ray through each cell. This figure is three-dimensional: drag the background to turn it and look at the rays from the side. Each cell of the screen is painted with what its ray found, and laid flat the cells are the picture in the corner.

Switch on Every ray to see all of them at once: a fan spreading out from one point, the eye, through the grid. Drag the circled cross on the screen to follow one pixel's ray, and change pixels across: the picture gets finer, and the number of rays grows with the square of it. Double the pixels across and down, and there are four times as many rays.

Everything else in this chapter is the same in 3D. The figures stay flat wherever a slice shows the idea more clearly, and turn three-dimensional where the third dimension is the point.

5What does the ray see?

When a ray hits something, the pixel needs to know how bright that point is. The simplest answer asks one more question: can this point see the lamp? Send a second ray from the hit point towards the lamp. If it gets there without hitting anything, the point is lit; if something is in the way, it is in shadow. This second ray is a shadow ray. Drag this pixel along the screen to follow a different ray.

How much light a lit point gets depends on two things. Distance: further from the lamp, less light. And angle: light arriving at a slant spreads over more surface, so it lights each bit of it less. The angle is measured from the surface's normal, the arrow pointing straight out of it. That is the cosine law.

The mathsHow bright a lit point is

A lamp of intensity I, at distance d from the point, seen at angle θ from the normal, gives the point this much light (its irradiance E):

E = I · cos θd   in the flat world,   E = I · cos θd2   in 3D

The difference is the shape light spreads over. In 3D, light from a lamp spreads over a sphere whose area grows with d²; in a flat world it spreads around a circle whose length grows with d. The figure uses the flat world's version, because that is the world it draws.

The pixel's brightness is then the surface's colour times E. Measuring light explains the units ShadyTracer gives these numbers.

Rays, a hit, a shadow ray to the lamp: that is already a working ray tracer, and the oldest kind. Its pictures look hard and dark, because it only counts light that comes straight from the lamp. A real room is lit just as much by light bouncing off its walls. The rest of this tutorial is about counting that light too (Following a path), and chapter 14 shows why the shadow ray is still the most useful trick in the whole renderer (Aim at the light).

6Edges, and jitter

A pixel covers a small wedge of directions, not one direction. But a ray through its middle looks in one direction only, and sees either the ball or the sky, never a bit of both. Along an edge that makes stair steps, aliasing. In the figure the ray goes through the middle every time: however many times you try, the pixel reads 0 or 1.

Now turn on Jitter. Each try (each sample) sends its ray through a random point inside the pixel. Some hit the ball and some miss, and the average of all of them settles on the share of the pixel the ball really covers. The edge comes out smooth.

ShadyTracer jitters every camera ray, every sample. That is why its picture starts rough and gets better while you watch: each frame adds more samples to each pixel's average. How fast an average settles, and how ShadyTracer knows how settled it is, is Dice and averages.

Move the edge slider to slide the ball's edge across the pixel, and press Start again.

8Tricks and trade-offs

    

      
One ray per pixel per sample, then average. Instead of tracing a perfect pixel once, ShadyTracer
        traces a rough one many times and keeps the average. The picture is usable after one sample and better after
        each one, and the camera can move at any time: the average just starts again.

    

    

      
The kernel that makes camera rays does not know the scene. It never reads the shapes, so its
        program text is the same for every scene, and the GPU's compiled copy is reused when you switch scenes
