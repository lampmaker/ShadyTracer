Following a path

Light reaches a pixel straight from a lamp, or after one bounce, or two, or ten. A path tracer follows one random route at a time, from the eye, bouncing off whatever it hits, and keeps one number along the way: how much of the light at the far end would still make it back. This chapter follows that number, says what is lost by stopping after a fixed number of bounces, and shows a fair way of stopping early.

1One path, bounce by bounce

A ray leaves the eye and hits a wall. The wall is matte: it scatters light in every direction, so the path picks one at random (more often near straight out of the wall, as chapter 8 explains) and carries on. It hits something else, and bounces again, until it happens to hit the lamp. What comes back is the lamp's light, but not all of it: each wall the path bounced off keeps part of the light, here 20%, and passes on its albedo, 0.8. So the path keeps a running product, its throughput, written β ("beta"): 1 on the way out of the eye, 0.8 after the first wall, 0.64 after the second, and so on. When the path finds light, it adds β times that light. This path bounced four times and found the lamp carrying β = 0.41.

Press Another path a few times: most go on bouncing for a long while before they find the lamp, and each brings back a different amount. The pixel's value is their average (chapter 12).

The mathsThe path's estimate

A path with bounces at points 1, 2, … k that ends on a light of radiance Le returns

L = βk Le,   βk = ∏i=1…k fi cos θipi

f
how much light the surface sends from the new direction towards the old (its BSDF, chapter 8)
cos θ
the slant the light arrives at, as in chapter 1
p
how likely the random direction was to be picked (its probability density)

For a matte surface of albedo ρ with directions picked in proportion to cos θ, every factor is exactly ρ: the formula's f cos θ and p cancel all but the albedo. Averaged over many paths, these estimates add up the light arriving after any number of bounces: the rendering equation, solved one random path at a time.

2Where to stop

In a closed room a path never runs out of walls to hit, so a renderer has to stop it somewhere. The simplest rule is a cap: at most so many bounces. Whatever light the path would have found after that is lost. This figure averages many paths through the room and reads each one at every cap, from no bounce at all to 40, against the bold line, a cap of 40. With walls of albedo 0.8, a cap of 1 keeps 46% of the light, 3 keeps 72%, 8 keeps 95%.

White walls (0.95) give back more at every bounce, so more of the light comes late: a cap of 3 keeps only 46%, 8 keeps 76%. A cap makes the picture darker than it should be, and darkest where light bounces most (a white room, a corner, a light shining into a box). That is a bias: no number of samples brings it back. ShadyTracer's cap is 10 bounces by default, and its Render settings take it up to 40 (Max Bounces).

3Stopping fairly

After many bounces a path carries little: at β = 0.1 it can bring back a tenth of what it finds, and it costs as much to trace as a path at full strength. Russian roulette stops it early, fairly. After the third bounce, the path goes on with a probability p equal to its β (kept between 0.1 and 0.95), and stops otherwise. A path that goes on divides its β by p, so it carries more, exactly making up for its stopped companions: on average, nothing is lost. The plot shows how many paths end after each number of bounces, without roulette (thin) and with it (bold): with it, most paths end soon after the third bounce.

The readout compares the two over the same number of paths. The averages agree within their error bars. With roulette a path costs 4.7 rays instead of 10.0, and its result is noisier; for the same number of rays, the error bar comes out a little smaller (0.96 of the other's). In this room, roulette is a small win; how small or large depends on the scene.

The mathsWhy nothing is lost

A path whose remaining light would be X is replaced by

X′ = X / p with probability p,   0 otherwise

and the average of X′ is p · X/p + (1 − p) · 0 = X: the same as before, for any p above 0. Only the spread grows, and it grows least when the paths that stop are those that carried little anyway, which is why p follows β. The floor of 0.1 keeps a survivor from carrying more than ten times its β: one lucky survivor of a very weak path would otherwise be a bright speck (a firefly).

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A brightening that was the measurement. Switching roulette on once brightened the picture
        by 6 to 8%, and the comment recorded it as unexplained, naming its own suspect: the renderer adapts how many
        rounds a sample gets, and roulette, by ending paths early, changed that, so the two runs were not given the same
        work. With the adapting pinned off, the gap vanished (the measurement above). The lesson the repository drew:
        compare two settings only with everything else held still.
    • 
      
  • Roulette written and switched off. It was in the code, commented out, so every path ran to the
        cap however little it carried.
    • 
      
  • A clamp that hid a wrong formula. Each bounce's weight used to be clamped to [0, 10], to cut
        bright specks at glancing angles. The specks came from a wrong probability for the direction drawn; with that
        fixed, a reflection's weight is bounded by the formula itself (at most 1 over the chance of picking that kind of
        bounce), and the clamp, which would only have biased the picture, is gone.
