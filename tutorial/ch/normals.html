Which way is up

Once a ray has found a surface, almost everything that happens next depends on which way that surface faces: how much light falls on it, where a bounce goes, how glass bends the ray. A distance field knows the answer without being told, because the direction straight out of a surface is the direction its distance grows fastest. Finding it costs either a few lines of maths per shape, or a few more looks at the whole scene per hit.

1Uphill, by looking around

The normal is the arrow straight out of a surface, one unit long. In a distance field, it is the way the distance grows fastest, straight across the lines of equal distance: the field's gradient. For a circle that is the direction from its middle to the point, one line of maths, and many shapes give theirs like that, with their distance (the arrow marked exact). For any other, the renderer finds it by differences: it asks the distance a small step h away, once to the right and once up (the dots), and sees how much it grew each way. Those two changes, as an arrow, point uphill (forward). Asking a step either side in each direction, four looks instead of two, gives central differences.

With a large step, both are a little off on a curved surface: forward by 3.9°, central by 0.6° (the readout). The surface bends within the step. Shrink the step and both close in on the exact arrow: forward's error shrinks as fast as the step, central's as fast as the step squared, so central is far better for the same step and costs twice the looks.

Near a crease, where the circle meets the box, a step can land on the other shape. Here the probe is on the circle, a short way from the crease: the central differences' looks to the left and below land in the box's field, and their arrow leans 20° towards the box. The forward looks go right and up, away from the box, and stay within 7°.

With a step a tenth as big, no look reaches the box, and both are right again. A differences normal blurs every crease over about one step's width; with ShadyTracer's step, a hair's breadth (section 3), nobody sees it.

The mathsForward and central differences

nforward ∝ ( f(p + h x̂) − f(p), f(p + h ŷ) − f(p), … )

ncentral ∝ ( f(p + h x̂) − f(p − h x̂), … )

f(p)
the scene's distance at the point; the renderer already has it from the march
x̂, ŷ
one unit along each axis (and ẑ in 3D, the "…")
∝
"points the same way as": the arrow is then made one unit long

Forward needs one new look per axis, 3 in 3D; central needs two, 6 in 3D. Their errors on a smooth surface shrink like h and like h² (a Taylor series says why). Forward differences also lean: they look only one way along each axis, which is why, near the crease, they missed the box that central ones found. Quilez's article below shows a third way, four looks at the corners of a tetrahedron: even-handed like central differences, for four looks instead of six.

2Too small a step

So the smaller the step, the better? Not on a GPU. Its numbers are 32-bit (f32): about seven significant digits. At a metre from the scene's origin, two neighbouring f32 numbers are 0.12 µm apart, one spacing, and a distance can only change in such jumps. This figure does the sums the way the GPU does, every one rounded to f32, for a circle of radius a tenth of a metre, a metre from the origin. On the left it is lit through its normals by differences, and each normal's error is drawn as a tick outside it, longer for a larger error; on the right, the worst error around the circle against the step. At 128 spacings it is 2.2 milliradians (an eighth of a degree), and the ticks are too short to see.

At 2 spacings, the change the differences measure is a few jumps, and rounding decides it: the normal is off by up to 110 mrad (6°), and the ticks are a ragged fringe, different from one point to the next, a pattern made by f32 rather than by the shape. On a shiny surface that is grain in the highlight. It is the red curve, falling as the step grows.

At 16,384 spacings, 2 mm, the rounding is gone, but the step is large enough for the circle to bend within it: the error of section 1 (the soft curve, rising). Now the ticks are smooth, the same at neighbouring points. The best step sits between the two.

A shape much smaller than its distance from the origin is different: a circle of radius one millimetre, a metre away. Its own numbers are small, so f32's rounding hardly shows at any step, but at 128 spacings, 15 µm, the step is a fair part of its size, and its normal is off by 7.7 mrad, smoothly.

5Tricks and trade-offs

    
Each is stamped with how it is known; point at a stamp to see what it means.

    

      
A shape's own normal is worth a lot. With the shapes' own normals switched off
        (fastnormals 0, a developer setting), every hit takes its normal by differences, and a frame took
        4.3 times as long: 63.6 ms a sample against 14.8, on the user's Pascal card (docs/TESTING.md, "Where
        the render time goes"; the setting's own comment in uniforms.schema.js). The setting's label says
        so: off = 4.3x slower.

    

    

      
Why that much is not known. Three more looks per hit, against a march of dozens of looks to find
        it, would suggest a few percent, not four times. That count is a guess about what costs what, which this
        repository's rules forbid treating as a fact; the 4.3 is the fact. The renderer can time each of its kernels on
        the GPU (core/kerneltime.js, used by the Tests bench); timed with the setting on and off, it would
        show which kernel grows, and that would say why.

    

    

      
A normal left out on purpose. The cone's exact distance comes from Quilez's list, but its
        normal is left to the renderer: "the exact gradient has four regions, and a normal that is wrong in one of them is
        worse than a slower right one"
        (). Each newer shape's normal is
        held to central differences by core/test/shapes.test.mjs.

    

    

      
Bent space throws normals away. A twist or bend (chapter 3) zeroes every shape's normal inside
        it, since the shapes' own arrows were worked out in unbent space; the renderer then takes them by differences of
        the bent field, which are right.

    

  






  

    
6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • A normal that was not one unit long. The GPU's sign(0) is 0, which made normals
        of the wrong length on an axis (chapter 2); the newer shapes use a comparison instead.
    • 
      
  • A normal that was not a number deleted its surface: making a zero arrow one unit long divides
        by zero, and the surface then fails the facing test every surface goes through (chapter 3). Every normalize in the
        newer shapes is guarded.
    • 
      
  • The warped fillet: a blend made its normal from one shape's arrow and another's zero
        (chapter 3). The fix was to give no normal at all, and let differences find it.
    • 
      
  • Six looks nobody asked for. A central-difference normal sat in the shader beside the forward
        one, uncalled, until version 0.22.3 (the comment above calcNormals_fast).
