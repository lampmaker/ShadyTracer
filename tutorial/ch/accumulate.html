Part VI · The machine · Chapter 23

Adding up light on a GPU

A path that ends hands its light to its pixel; so does a shadow ray that reaches the sun, and a photon's connection to the camera. In one sample many of them land on the same pixel, from threads running at the same moment, and the sum must come out right. Then the sample is folded into the picture's average, and a picture too big for the card's buffers is traced in pieces that must add up to one sample. This chapter is those three, and the bugs each one had.

1Many hands on one pixel

Six threads each add some light to one pixel, 26 in all. The plain way is two steps: load the pixel's number, then store it plus your own. In one group in lock-step every thread loads first, all reading 0, then each stores its own value over the others: the pixel ends at 2, the last thread's, and 24 of the 26 are lost. Point at a box to follow one thread.

Threads of different groups interleave at no set pace, and whether a load and its store have another thread's store between them is luck: here the pixel ends at 10. ShadyTracer once folded each path into the picture this way, a load, the arithmetic and a store, and lost updates; with one ray a pixel no two threads ever met at a pixel, which hid it (section 6).

An atomic add is one step no other thread can come between: 26, every time. WGSL has atomic adds for whole numbers, not for floating-point numbers, so ShadyTracer's frame buffer once held whole numbers: fixed point, 65,536 to a unit of light (section 2).

For a float, a compare-and-swap: load the number, work out the new one, and write it only if the pixel still holds what was loaded; if not, take what it does hold and try again. Every thread's light is counted, at a price when they crowd one pixel. In this figure's model of one group, the swaps on one address go one after another, so one succeeds a round and the others try again: 15 failed swaps for 6 threads.

Sixteen threads on one pixel: 120 failed swaps, 17 rounds where an atomic add takes one. In the model the failures grow as the square of the crowd, n(n − 1)/2. Paths spread over a picture rarely crowd a pixel; light tracing's connections pile into the few pixels of a caustic (chapter 18), and section 5 has a measurement that may be this.

2Numbers that fit the light

The old frame buffer kept light as whole numbers, 65,536 to a unit, so it could use the integer atomic add. A contribution of 0.3 comes out as 0.29998779, off by about one part in 25,000: fine while the light was a number near 1.

Then ShadyTracer moved to real units (chapter 5), and a sunlit white wall is about 25,000 cd/m². A pixel's 32-bit word could hold at most 65,535 a sample, and several contributions add up, so each was clamped at 10,000 to keep the word from wrapping round to small numbers: the wall lost 60% of its light. An f32 (a 32-bit float) keeps 25,000 exactly.

The sun's disc is about 1.6 × 109 cd/m²: clamped to 10,000, all but six parts in a million gone. These limits came from the number format, not from anything physical.

And at the other end, anything under 1/65,536 was cut to 0: a faint contribution of a millionth vanished whole. An f32 keeps every value on this line to about seven significant digits. Drag the contribution along the line. So the frame buffer holds the bits of f32s now, added by the compare-and-swap loop of section 1.

3Bigger than the buffer

A sample traces a camera ray for every pixel and every photon the light tracer asked for, and the rays in flight must fit the ray buffers: 1 GiB, 496 bytes a ray (two ray buffers and a shade queue item), 2,164,802 rays. At 1280 × 720, 921,600 camera rays and a million photons fit at once: one batch. The batches' plan here is ShadyTracer's own function, run in this page.

At 3840 × 2160, 8,294,400 camera rays and a million photons are 5 batches. Each batch is a fair slice of every stream (1,658,880 camera rays and 200,000 photons each), runs the whole wavefront chain of chapter 22, and leaves its light in the frame buffer; the picture's average is updated once, after the last. Each photon's light is divided by the sample's whole count, not the batch's, so the batches add up to one sample: the picture's size changes how long a sample takes, and nothing else.

With the photon map on (chapter 19), every photon of a sample must have landed before the first camera ray gathers, so the photons go in batches of their own, first: 2 batches where 1 would fit.
