Part III · What surfaces do · Chapter 10

Glass

Glass, water, acrylic and diamond let light through, bend it as it crosses their surface, and reflect a little of it, more at a slant. ShadyTracer has an optics bench for LED and luminaire design, where all of that has to be exact. This chapter is the two laws involved (Snell's and Fresnel's), what happens inside a slab and a ball, how the renderer knows which material a ray is inside, and how coloured glass absorbs.

1Bending and reflecting

Light slows down in glass. How much is its index of refraction n: 1 for air, 1.33 for water, about 1.5 for glass, 2.42 for diamond. Crossing into a slower material, a ray bends towards the normal, by Snell's law: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2. Here light from the air meets glass at 45° (drag it) and bends to 28.13°. Some reflects instead: 5.0% here. How much is Fresnel's law, the chart on the right: about 4% straight on, rising to all of it at grazing.

Light is a wave that vibrates across its direction, and the two ways it can vibrate (across the plane of the drawing, s, and in it, p) reflect differently: s 9.2%, p 0.8%. Sunlight and lamplight mix both equally, so a renderer can use their average (unpolarised, the bold curve). At one angle p does not reflect at all (marked); that is why polarising sunglasses cut glare off water.

From inside the glass out, at 30°, the ray bends away from the normal, to 48.59°.

At 50° there is no angle to bend to: sin θ2 would have to be 1.15. Past the critical angle, 41.81° for glass, all the light reflects: total internal reflection, which keeps light inside an optical fibre and makes the far side of an aquarium a mirror.

The mathsFresnel's two reflectances

With η = n1/n2, the cosine of the incoming angle c1, and of the refracted one c2 = √(1 − η²(1 − c1²)) (Snell's law, written for cosines):

rs = η c1 − c2η c1 + c2,   rp = c1 − η c2c1 + η c2,   R = ½(rs² + rp²)

When 1 − η²(1 − c1²) is negative there is no c2: total internal reflection, R = 1. Straight on, both are ((n − 1)/(n + 1))², 0.04 for n = 1.5. rp is 0 where tan θ1 = n2/n1, Brewster's angle.

2A pane, every bounce counted

A pane of glass has two surfaces, and light reflects at each, back and forth inside. At 45° each face reflects 5.0%: 90.2% of the light gets through on its first pass, and each later pass adds a little, to 90.4% in all, which is (1 − R)/(1 + R). The renderer does not add the series up: each path takes one branch at each face, reflected with chance R, and the average over many paths is the sum.

The renderer averages s and p afresh at every surface. Real light keeps its polarisation from one face to the next, and the exact answer averages the two series instead: 90.7% here. The optics module's own note gives the model's error: none straight on, 0.3% at 45°, 3% at 70°.

At 78° each face reflects 32.6%, and the later passes matter: 45.5% through on the first, 50.9% in all.

Coloured glass absorbs as light crosses it, more the further it goes: Beer–Lambert's law. ShadyTracer gives a coloured material its colour and a colour depth, the distance at which that much of each channel is left. With a colour of 0.5 at a depth of one pane's thickness, the slanted path across keeps 45.6%; through it all, 41.1%, and 52.9% of the light is absorbed.

3A glass ball

Parallel light through a glass ball, as from the sun: each ray bends in at the front and out at the back, towards the axis. Rays near the axis meet 1.5 radii from the centre, half a radius behind the ball; the outer ones cross nearer, at 1.104 for a ray 0.9 out. That spread is why a ball makes a blurred focus, and on a floor under it a bright caustic ring and spot (chapter 18 is how the renderer finds those).

Water bends less: its focus is 2.015 radii out.

Diamond bends so much that its near-axis rays meet inside the ball. Rays near its edge arrive inside past the critical angle and go round, reflected, before they leave.

6What went wrong before

    
    
      
  • Every glass reflected 4%. Fresnel came from a "specular" slider, never from the index: "water,
        glass and diamond all reflected 4%, and total internal reflection jumped from 4% to 100%"
        (microfacet.js's header).
    • 
      
  • Rough glass that never let light out past the critical angle. It chose to reflect by the
        Fresnel at the surface's overall angle, 1 past the critical angle, though tilted facets still let light through:
        a rough glass sphere lost 8% in the furnace. It now draws a facet first and decides by that facet's Fresnel
        (Walter et al., 2007; the comment in lobesSample).
    • 
      
  • A mirror under water. An opaque surface used to ask the scene what was beyond it too, which
        "turned an IOR-1 default material met from inside water into a total-internal-reflection mirror" (section 4's
        excerpt).
    • 
      
  • Thick and thin glass alike. Before colour depth (version 0.37.0), a coloured material tinted a
        ray at every crossing, so "two boxes of one transmissive material, one thicker, transmitted the same"
        (CHANGELOG.md).
    • 
      
  • A glass ball with a plain shadow. The sun through glass was counted by neither of chapter 15's
        two ways (its story there).
